Friday 16 October

Welcome & Year in Review
⚡ All togetherFriday 16 October

What this session was

The opening session of ALM 2026 — a welcome, a practical briefing, and a warm-up to get everyone oriented before the working weekend began.

What happened

Welcome

Daniela welcomed attendees and introduced the org team: Doreen (venue), Yllza (meals & shifts), Yakir and Martian (equipment & tech), Céline and Daniela (programme), Cupcake (ALM finance and travel reimbursements), and Leaf/Frank supporting remotely during the weekend. Doreen ran through house rules for the Vidalia space.

Lessons Learned

Céline opened a short lessons-learned round, drawing on the Discord channel and inviting the room to share experiences from 2026. Stories ranged from “emergency mud baths” and lost Jeep keys to sound policy breaches by a certain Trojan horse; setting a candid, energetic tone for the weekend.

Annual Roadmap framework

Daniela then presented the proposed Annual Roadmap framework: a film-industry-inspired production stage model (Development → Pre-production → On-site → Post-event) with hard milestones: event dates confirmed and budget voted by December, tickets on sale in January, barrio placement moving earlier in the year. This generated good discussion, particularly around how early barrio leads could realistically engage and how earlier ticket sales would help cash flow.

Finance Overview

Peter gave a Finance Overview of the 2026 cycle: ~5,000 transactions, a 20% margin in the first year as a new entity, and a cash flow crunch caused by Stripe holding a significant portion of revenue until after the event. A new automated finance system (expense reports → approval → accounting → bank) is now in place for 2027. The Finance Working Group kicks off the following Monday, open to anyone who wants a say in the budget.

Assets Working Group

Ellen gave a short update from the Assets Working Group on storage and equipment for the 2027 event. No decisions were taken; questions go through the working group.

Facilitation Guidelines briefing

Cupcake and Ellen closed with a Facilitation Guidelines briefing for the weekend’s sessions: roles (facilitator, secretary/timekeeper, hybrid guardian), five principles (autonomy, speak with intention, one point per person, speak for yourself, be for not against), hand signals for speaking order, clarifications, disagreement, and temperature checks, plus a consensus model (agree / agree with reservations / block) rather than majority voting.

Outcomes / decisions

  • Annual Roadmap stage framework proposed; turned into a Gantt chart post-ALM
  • Finance Working Group confirmed, starting Monday 19 October, open to all
  • Assets Working Group update shared
  • Facilitation framework distributed to all session leads for the weekend
Working Group Speed Dating
⚡ All togetherFriday 16 October

What this session was

A rapid-fire pitch round for all the weekend’s working sessions. Each person got a couple of minutes to explain what their session was about and what they needed from participants, so people could make an informed choice about where to spend their time.

Sessions pitched

Event-Time Ops & Org Hub

Working group: Event Ops

A proposal to design a dedicated physical hub for event-time operations, somewhere people can come with questions, needs, and emergencies, rather than routing everything through Malfare or calling coordinators directly. The session would map what that hub looks like, what it covers, and how handovers and shifts work from it.

Beyond Safehaus: What Should Crew Rest Look Like?

Working group: Crew Rest

A rethink of how the organisation provides rest for its crew across all phases: setup, event, and strike. This year’s AC cabins reduced the need for some volunteers to leave site; the traditional Safehaus inadequacy brought questions about what we need from that space; and the strike decompression rotation shone a light on a possibility for a different resting need post-event. The session would explore formalising that model: dedicated sleeping configurations in cabins, bunk setups, and making the decompression space an official, rotational part of the strike plan.

Clearing the Air on Sound

Working group: Sound Policy

The sound policy needs an update as it touches almost every area of the event: power, malfare, barrio support, creativity, art cars. The session would work through a structured list of outstanding questions: barrio sound rules, art piece and art car policy, year-on-year continuity of enforcement, and how to support nomads navigating sound complaints. Goal: a solid enough discussion to produce a formal proposal before city planning kicks off barrio placement.

Designing Our Site: Org Footprint, Structures and Spaces

Working group: Site Design

A proposal to collectively design the site, starting with the organisation’s structures; doing now in the development phase, rather than after barrio placement during the pre-production phase. This year, things like the Malfare sleeping cabins ending up next to Werkhaus speakers only became obvious once everything was built. The session would gather department-by-department spatial and structural needs so city planning, infrastructure, and power can design the city around the org, not the other way around.

MoE/MoN: What Are We Building?

Working group: MoE Improvements

MoN has ambitions to be the beating heart of the event, but the equipment is outdated and the vision needs refreshing. The session would explore what it would take — new kit, new concept — to make that a reality.

How We’re Structured: Roles, Responsibilities & Reporting

Working group: Organisational Structure

A structured session based on the survey sent to leads, coordinators, and board members. Topics: department definitions, lead and coordinator roles, board roles, selection and deselection processes, and payroll transparency. The aim is to come out with formal definitions and agreed processes, not just a discussion.

Policy around Politics: Working Group proposal

Working group: to be formed

A first conversation about how the organisation handles political questions: pro/contra positions from attendees, pronoun signage objections, geopolitical tensions on site. No solutions are expected in this slot; the goal is to start a working group that can think of ways to give leads in roles that handle these situations —mainly the Participant Wellness teams— clear definitions of their role’s scope and guardrails for how to deal with it.

Werkhaus: From Volunteer Camp to Org Infrastructure

Working group: Werkhaus

Werkhaus sits in a blurry space between the organisation and a barrio, which caused problems this year: fluff volunteer confusion, ice budget disputes, and almost no one stepping up to run the barrio until the last minute. The session would try to draw clearer lines: what’s the org’s responsibility, what’s the barrio’s, and how do we make sure it’s properly staffed in advance.

New Site: Purchase and Infrastructure

Working group: ElSitio (New Site)

A department-by-department input session on what a site move would actually mean: costs of projects, potential savings, and practical considerations from the people on the ground. The current assumption is renting the same site for June 2027; but if we were to try the New Site, the decision needs to be made by December. This session feeds the business case.

How Sunday closes

Each session lead will give a brief outcome update in the closing plenary on Sunday morning. Attendees are asked to check out with the venue team before leaving. The ALM feedback survey goes out Monday around noon.

Saturday 17 October

Event-Time Ops & Org Hub
⚡ All togetherSaturday 17 October

What this session was

A working group to design a dedicated Event Operations Hub for 2027: a physical space and role that takes over the shift check-in, check-out, and handover functions that currently fall by default on Malfare, Production, and Onsite Coordinators.

The problem it’s solving

Right now, when volunteers show up to start or finish a shift, there’s no clear place to go or a clear person to receive them. They end up at Werkhaus, Malfare, or Production depending on who they find first. Malfare in particular absorbs a huge amount of this. Radio handovers happening in the same space where sensitive conversations are taking place. Coordinators get pulled in constantly for things that aren’t strictly their job. The goal is to give this its own home and role.

What was agreed

Location

The hub will sit within the existing Welfare Compound. It will share a front-of-house space with Malfare (keeping the visible, welcoming face people already know) but have a separate office for operational work, so Malfare’s private and sensitive space stays protected. Being inside the compound also means the role isn’t isolated at night and can draw on the collective knowledge of the people working nearby. Signage across the whole compound needs a significant upgrade: big, visible, lit at night, with clear directional arrows to Event Ops Hub, Welfare, Malfare, Mission Control, and Safer Space.

Physical setup

An AC cabin (required for radio storage) with a shaded outdoor area. A lockbox system for after-hours access to kits; as used by Kunsthaus and in the Production Offices this year. A freezer and shelf for volunteer fluff.

What the hub manages

Check-in and check-out for all event-time volunteer shifts, with a printed checklist for each role. Distribution of radios, kits, vouchers, fluff boxes and shift binders. Tracking who showed up, who was late, and flagging gaps. A charging station for radios, with spare day-to-day radios on hand; main radio stock and batteries remain in the Radio cabin.

The role

The existing Radio Ranger role will be repurposed and expanded into this Event Ops role, taken out of Radios entirely so the radio team can focus on the radios. Ideally staffed by two people given the volume and spread of handovers. They carry a radio and are on call between handover windows. Radio Rangers’ current responsibilities for giving nomads their bags, vouchers, and binders fold naturally into this.

Shift standardisation

Full standardisation isn’t realistic given the range of departments, but shifts can be staggered so handovers are spread across a window rather than all hitting at once. Some flexibility is essential: Safer Space runs two-hour shifts for accessibility reasons; departments need to be able to hold nomads over during incidents. The rough overnight handover picture from Humans: 11pm welfare lead, midnight welfare, 2am nomads and interpreters, 4am welfare, 7am welfare shift lead, 8am nomads and power. Post-ALM action: map all event-time shifts in full to design the hub’s actual operating rhythm. Event Ops shifts would need to start between shifts, not along with others, to allow some time for landing.

Scope boundary

This role manages shifts, not the jobs within them. They are not following the water truck or doing welfare briefings. Departmental shift leads still brief their own people; this role handles the logistics wrapper around that.

What’s still open

  • Full shift map across all event-time departments (post-ALM)
  • Whether the role needs to be formally 24/7 or can operate on a staffed-plus-on-call model
  • Fallback coverage if the role no-shows (radio team as backstop was suggested)
  • Humans feature request: ability to mark volunteer no-shows in real time, and an on-call notification system so available people can be pinged when a gap appears mid-event

Next step

Daniela to write up a document proposal for circulation and comment. Physical placement to be worked through in the Site Design session.

Beyond Safehaus: What Should Crew Rest Look Like?
ParallelSaturday 17 October

What this session was

A working group to redesign how the organisation provides rest for its setup, event, and strike crew: moving away from the safehaus model toward something more intentional, more self-managed, and more restorative.

The problem

The current safehaus setup is operationally draining to manage. The vendor is unreliable and volatile. Transportation to and from site creates its own friction: people who left to relax came back more stressed. The roles managing it have bounced between Volunteer Coordination and Production with neither fully wanting to own it, and nobody wanting to repeat the job.

On the upside: AC cabins on site this year made a real difference. People slept better, both at night and during siesta, and the general energy on site was noticeably good. The strike decompression rotation —people who had left, rested properly somewhere else, and then came back fresh— also worked really well.

What was agreed

The group landed on a hybrid model built around four components:

On-site sleeping cabins

Two dedicated rest cabins, each with two bunk beds (four people per cabin, eight total). AC is required — which means the power load needs to be planned from the start. A phased cabin power-up plan should be built alongside crew arrival schedules so consumption is managed. A shade structure over the cabins was floated as a way to reduce the cooling load. These cabins need clear rules, a booking/allocation system, and a lighting signal (red = do not disturb) to help the “don’t bother people who are resting” norm stick.

Hotel rooms in town

Eight allocated rooms (plus two held back as emergency buffer) at a hotel in Sariñena, for people who need off-site separation and time alone — a private room, a proper shower, a door that shuts. The hotel should ideally offer a laundry service so people aren’t making separate trips. No communal safehaus space is needed: once Werkhaus is up early (as it was this year), on-site social space exists and people stopped wanting to leave.

A bus route

The biggest unlock. A regular minibus run — probably three times a day, stopping at the org area, gate, the pool, town, and the train station — removes the current dependency on volunteer drivers and on coordinators having to manage ad hoc lifts. Daniela is pursuing this with the comarca president, who has indicated the municipality has a minibus available for this kind of use. If this happens, people become self-reliant for transport and the management burden drops significantly.

Strike decompression

The same hotel logic applied to strike: a block of allocated spots at a decompression site about an hour from site, rotated across strike volunteers so everyone gets a couple of days off. Followed by a shared decompression for the core strike team at the end — one or two days together before everyone disperses.

What’s still open

Budget

All of this will cost more than the current safehaus (~€2k). Daniela to cost it up and bring it to the Finance Working Group as a line item. The argument: well-rested volunteers stay longer, perform better, and come back next year.

Allocation system

The rough formula is one rest day per week on site, pre-allocated and visible in Humans, with the option to self-manage trades. Pre-event and first-crew blocks of volunteers should get their rest days mapped before they arrive, not ad hoc on site. Nurz flagged that some people will need emergency headroom beyond their allocation — illness, intense job blocks — so the system needs buffer built in.

Who owns the admin

This has historically fallen between Volunteer Coordination and Production. It needs a clear owner, and the goal is to make Humans do the heavy lifting so neither department is manually managing beds.

Local vendor relationships

Laundry access, local sourcing, and the post office/reception space concept (using the former “unsafehaus” premises as an org base in town for the pre-event period) are adjacent ideas to explore.

Power planning for cabins

Needs to be integrated into the phased site power-up schedule.

Clearing the Air on Sound
ParallelSaturday 17 October

What this session was

A working group to begin updating the sound policy; a document that touches almost every area of the event. The session worked through the main problem areas: barrio zones, art pieces, art cars, setup and strike, enforcement, and incident recording. No policy was decided; the output is a working group mandate and a roadmap to a draft proposal by December.

Why this needs updating

The current sound policy exists; there’s a map, a section in the Barrio Guide, and a written escalation process, but it isn’t working in practice. Complaints aren’t systematically recorded. Departments and roles that touch sound (Power, Creativity, Barrio Support, Malfare, Site Lead) aren’t sharing information with each other in any structured way. And the regulations for camps, art installations, and art cars are inconsistent enough that it’s unclear what the rules actually are for each category.

Barrio sound zones

The zone system as it stands isn’t doing what it’s supposed to. Almost no barrios registered in green; most piled into yellow, which became a catch-all that means different things to different people. The decibel numbers in the current policy aren’t intuitive; most people can’t internalise what 80dB means without a reference point. The suggestion of using plain-language descriptions (“you can hold a conversation five metres from the speakers”) rather than raw figures was broadly supported as something people can check without equipment.

A few structural ideas were discussed:

The zone count

Somewhere between three and five zones seems right: three is too coarse, five is too granular, and the transitions between zones are currently too abrupt. Sound barriers (hay bales were mentioned as a practice at other burns) could soften those transitions, particularly if the organisation ever owns land.

Time-variable limits

Rather than a single decibel level per zone for the whole week, the idea of different permitted levels at different times was floated; a “wildcard night” where a camp in yellow can act as orange for one agreed evening, is an example. No consensus on the mechanism, but the principle of flexibility around peak nights resonated.

A shared party space in the red zone

Several camps want to host a big party but don’t want to be placed in red for the whole event. A designated party area in the red periphery, bookable by camps for individual nights, and speakers pointing outward, would let them have their party without permanently camping in the loudest zone. This is already happening informally; the proposal is to formalise it.

Art pieces and art cars

The core problem is that sound information for art pieces isn’t collected consistently, isn’t shared across departments, and often isn’t available even to the artists themselves. Some installations only reveal their true sound output once they’re built and running on site.

The proposal is a uniform set of four questions asked of all sound-producing submissions (barrios, art pieces, and art cars alike):

  1. Do you have a sound system?
  2. What size is it?
  3. What are you planning to do with it?
  4. How do you plan to mitigate your impact on others, particularly during quiet hours?

Answers go into Humans and feed directly into city planning and placement decisions, which means the information needs to be collected by the end of December so planning can start in January.

For art cars specifically, a registration-day sound check was proposed; art mobiles already gather at a fixed location on Tuesday afternoon for safety checks, and adding a sound check at that point is feasible. Limiters (small devices added to the speaker signal line) were discussed as a way to enforce limits technically rather than relying on self-regulation. Some burns do this routinely.

Art cars also need clearer zone mapping: the event map currently only colour-codes barrios and free-camping areas, not the open playa. A version showing which zones art cars can play in, and designated spots where large sound systems can park and point outward, would help drivers know where they are.

One open problem: unattended installations. If a piece is playing through the night and the artist isn’t there, there’s currently no reliable way to contact them. The suggestion is that every sound-producing installation should have a physical address or contact point on the piece itself, not a QR code (artists and participants may not have phones during the event), but a simple note: “Find us at [camp/location].”

Enforcement and sanctions

The session spent significant time on this, because enforcement is where sound policy collapses. The current escalation: Nomad → Nomad Lead → Site Lead unplugs; exists in principle but not in practice, and depends entirely on whoever is available at the time having enough authority and confidence to act.

A few things were agreed in principle:

  • Nomads keep their trusted role. They shouldn’t become the enforcement arm; people go to them because they feel safe doing so, and that needs to be protected. Nomads receive complaints and attempt mediation; that doesn’t change.
  • When mediation fails, escalation needs a clear owner. The proposal is Nomad → Nomad Lead → Production, with Production making the decision and Power carrying out any physical action (unplugging). Going through Production also creates a record.
  • Year-round accountability matters more than on-site intervention. Trying to enforce limits mid-party, with a volunteer who’s been on shift for two hours, against a barrio lead who’s been doing similar events for fifteen years, is structurally weak. The more effective lever is what happens afterward: incidents recorded during the event feed into decisions the following year about art grants, art-mobile licences, and barrio placement. Withholding next year’s grant application or restricting a sound system to a specific zone are levers the organisation already has, it just isn’t using them.

The consent response process was raised as the closest existing model. It has a year-round structure for collecting incidents, addressing them, and following up; something the sound policy entirely lacks. The working group will speak with the Consent team about how that process works and take inspiration from it.

Sanctions should be tied to individuals, not projects, where possible. The people involved in an art piece or art car can change year to year; repeated behaviour from the same person should carry consequences regardless of which project they’re attached to.

Setup and strike

Setup and strike have no clear sound policy at all: parties happen, but nobody’s agreed when they should stop or what the rules are. The tension is real: some people work at night and need mornings to sleep; others are awake at dawn and want music. Tool noise and amplified music are different problems but both cause friction.

The emerging position: if you want a party during setup or strike, talk to your neighbours first and agree a cut-off time. The session facilitator described doing exactly this during setup this year: agreeing a finish time with adjacent camps, being thanked for it, and building genuine trust as a result. That neighbourly negotiation model, rather than a blanket rule, seems like the most viable approach for a volunteer context.

A rough outer limit of 3am was floated as a reasonable default for amplified music during setup and strike. Not a decision, a question to bring back to the working group.

Roadmap

The facilitator’s proposed process from here:

  1. A first draft policy covering barrios, art pieces, and art cars in a single unified document.
  2. The draft to be circulated to barrio leads, art installation leads, and asociados for feedback.
  3. One or two community calls to collect wider input.
  4. A revised proposal, aiming to be voted on by the asociados by end of December, so that city planning can begin barrio placement in January with the policy in place.

Join the working group on Discord to stay involved.

Designing Our Site: Org Footprint, Structures and Spaces
ParallelSaturday 17 October

What this session was

A working group to begin collective, intentional site design for 2027; gathering department-by-department spatial and infrastructure needs early in the development phase, so city planning, infrastructure, and power can design the city around the organisation rather than retrofitting it afterwards.

Why now

This year, site layout decisions got made too late or by default. Malfare’s sleeping cabins ended up next to Werkhaus speakers. Generators blocked the front of Malfare from the playa. Barrio Support was too central during setup and left an ugly gap once the event started. The proposal is to fix the org footprint first, then build the barrio map around it instead of the other way round.

Department needs gathered

Production

Needs to be adjacent to the vendors’ road: first stop for trucks, deliveries, and authorities, without routing them through the event. Requires open space for vehicle access and parking, an AC office for computer work, and a meeting room for legal or authority visits. Open to sharing office space with other departments rather than maintaining separate cabins. Floated the idea of a shared “Production Village” compound with hot-desking, printing, and supply storage for multiple departments.

Power

Generators need to be placed as close to equidistant from consumption points as possible, fenced (flammable), and accessible by road for diesel trucks and forklifts. Miss Piggy needs a dedicated fenced prep area with a worktable, shade, and tool storage for building distro boxes. The solar gate area has the same road access and cabin requirements. Power currently shares cabin space with Production for printing and plan updates, happy to continue that. UPS units for Red Cross comms flagged as equipment need (not placement-critical).

Radios

Moving toward the Event Ops hub model, so no longer needs to be directly accessible from the playa. Needs an AC cabin space (lockable), close to the hub and Malfare. Happy to share the cabin with Geeks or finance; just needs desk space and a section for radio charging and day-to-day kit. The bulk of batteries and equipment stays in the Geeks’ cabin.

Barrio Support

Needs road access at the back for heavy equipment pickup (pallets, water cubes). Storage should be in a lockable container or taped-off area: equipment was being freely helped-to this year. Wants to be placed towards the back/road side rather than mid-playa, so the area doesn’t create an eyesore during the event. Needs a shaded outdoor area with seating for barrio registration groups, plus an AC cabin with internet and power for charging Makita batteries. Happy to share internet and printer access with Production if co-located. Ellen has a sketch showing how the compound could back onto the road.

Welfare Compound

Needs much more frontage: this year Ohana and a generator were in front of it, limiting visibility. Wants a wider open face toward the playa with clear signage and lighting for each space (Malfare, Welfare, Safer Space, Red Cross). AC cabin for leads’ office with secure storage for incident reports and confidential documents, plus AC sleep cabins nearby. Closer proximity to Red Cross (shared toilet access), and the Event Ops hub (for fluff volunteers). Welfare has its own container and needs to be near, but not necessarily front-and-centre. Closer toilets: they often use Red Cross’s, which isn’t ideal. Ohanahaus needs to be closer and have more space. Also Welfare needs more space. The year-before-last’s layout between Malfare and Werkhaus was flagged as a good reference to go back to.

Werkhaus Camping

Needs quiet in its sleeping area. The LNT container (Kermit) at the corner was waking campers with morning discussions. Kermit should move. Behind Container Village, between Container Village and Cantina or to Production Village, were suggested as better spots. Container orientation should be rotated 90° for better shade; this has been discussed before but didn’t make it onto site. Shower drainage also needs to be factored in.

Werkhaus Barrio

Possibly changing structure design next year (container vs rigging; still TBC with infra team). Needs clear playa frontage as a barrio, not buried in site infrastructure. Must be able to play music without disrupting Malfare. Sound zone: far end of Yellow. Proximity to Cantina important given the volume of movement between the two during setup and event. Internet access a nice-to-have for coordinators.

LNT HQ / Kermit Container

Happy to move away from Werkhaus Camping. Needs to be near Production and Toolhaus. Wants to be on the map so people can find it. Road access needed occasionally. No shower directly above it.

No Info / Ice Ice Baby

Currently well-placed relative to Production and Werkhaus; the proximity of this year works. Needs a visibility mechanism (flag or light) so people can see from the playa whether ice is available. More shade for volunteers. Rain protection over freezers. Power for freezers and cashless terminal. Wi-Fi for cashless terminal. Fixed, un-stealable seating for volunteers. Lockable AC cabin (shared with Barrio Support in event phase is viable). Phone charging point for participants. Desk space and locker inside.

Toilet design — Sanitation session (Shitshow)

A presentation on the vulva urinal trials in 2026. Two types were tested: squatting toilets (Lapees) and standing urinals (informally called ChatChichiPees). A post-event survey found both had advocates and detractors, with preferences varying by anatomy, height, and personal comfort. Key feedback: squatting units can spray; standing units need to be lower and wider; fencing felt intimidating especially at night; signage was unclear for many users.

Decisions: both types to continue in 2027, aiming for equal numbers of types of urinals. Improvements needed: lower and varied heights for the standing units, better splash design (angled pipe section suggested), friendlier enclosure materials (fabric over fencing), clearer signage, and a dedicated team that includes people who use them. Placement needs excavator involvement to pre-dig holes before site setup begins, which means it has to be planned early/exactly to avoid re-excavation. The sanitation company had no issues with the service access this year.

Structural decisions — open questions

The session closed with two discussion points that will need follow-up:

How do we decide to build something or not?

A prioritised list of must-haves is needed, with a calendar trigger — if a project isn’t confirmed by X date, it doesn’t get built. Everyone’s project feels essential, which makes ranking hard.

How do we separate working cabins from sleeping cabins?

Clear designation matters — people walking into a working cabin to find a person napping in it (or vice versa) creates friction and, in some cases, security risks for stored equipment. The proposal is explicit phase-based allocation: a cabin can be a rest cabin in week one, transition to an office as departments arrive, and potentially revert during strike. Shared AC space should be the default wherever security needs allow.

Accessibility and changing crew demographics were raised as a broader consideration — the organisation’s long-term volunteers are getting older, and site design should start actively accounting for mobility and health needs.

Next steps

  • Daniela to circulate a post-session survey to departments not represented at the session.
  • A follow-up call in a few weeks to consolidate the full needs list and begin mapping it into a city design document.

MoE/MoN: What Are We Building?
ParallelSaturday 17 October

What this session was

A working group to define what the Middle of Elsewhere is, what it could become, and what needs to happen between now and 2027 to get it there. Part vision, part practical scoping.

The middle of what?

The middle of elsewhere; a neutral ground in the middle of the chaos; a stage for those that may not fit in any camp’s theme; a shaded rest stop, in the middle of every shortcut between what and where.

The proposals

Outer structure

Iterate on the 2026 design with long-term re-usability, expandability, decor, lighting, and weather adaptability in mind. With a potential date shift to June, the structure needs to handle different elements; less extreme heat, more potential for rain and wind.

Inner structure

Integrate the Cookie Monster container into the backstage to protect equipment, allow easy access during the event, and double as a base for the stage roof. Better rain and wind protection for the stage, potentially including side walls.

Tech

Some existing equipment is out of order or unreliable in the desert environment. Some new equipment can be sourced at no cost or transport-only (audio mixer, wireless mics, cabling); stage lighting could be sourced at a discount by approaching manufacturers at ISE Europe. An LED strip around the outer structure and a light mounted high up would improve accessibility and recognisability of the space.

Decor and furniture

A budget should be proposed and secured early in the planning cycle rather than through an art grant. The space needs to transform quickly between uses —from rest and performance to workshops to late-night programming— so furniture should be modular, multifunctional, and easy to put away. More chests doubling as benches were suggested as a practical starting point.

Programme

Workshops and performances to be assigned slots based on their nature and requirements, respecting quiet times, with sound tech shifts timed accordingly (no amplification earlier in the day, limited during siesta—exact policy to be defined by community poll). An info pack, application process, and timeline need to be in place as art grants are announced in early 2027.

Next steps

  • Regular working group calls starting in November. A Telegram group will be set up alongside Discord so everyone can stay in the loop regardless of platform—all calendar invites posted in both, open to anyone who wants to get involved.
  • Barcelona storage inventory and equipment repairs to be scoped—Daniela and Viktor to sync on a possible date.
  • Space planning and documentation: an inventory and shared canvas for 3D planning are needed. Several departments flagged frustration with the lack of common planning tools; some prototypes are in development that could become part of Humans.

Policy around Politics: Working Group proposal
Open spaceSaturday 17 October

What this session was

A first conversation about how the organisation handles political questions on site — not to reach conclusions, but to map the terrain and form a working group. The session was called because these situations are already arriving at the inclusion team, and the goal is to have a framework before they escalate rather than after.

Why now

The inclusion team has been fielding an increasing number of politically charged situations: conflicts between participants from countries at war, questions about flags, art pieces with political content, and requests to the team that fall outside its actual remit. There is currently no written policy, no clear guidance for leads, and no agreed line between what is a legal question, what is an organisational principle, and what is a personal or barrio-level choice. Other burns have seen these tensions escalate badly. The goal is to get ahead of it.

The core distinction the session kept returning to

Several people pushed to separate three things that often get conflated:

What Spanish law determines

Some questions aren’t ours to decide; for example, whether the organisation can prohibit certain nationalities from attending. The law answers them. The policy needs to know where that line is.

What is an organisational principle

Things the organisation actively stands for: inclusion, non-discrimination, the code of conduct participants agree to when buying a ticket. These are already partially written; they need to be clearer and better communicated.

What is personal or barrio-level expression

Where individual or camp autonomy applies, and where the organisation has standing to intervene.

A secondary distinction was raised

The difference between political (relating to how society is organised—racism, rights, discrimination) and ideological (left/right, specific political movements). Not resolved, but flagged as something the working group needs to define clearly.

Three concrete areas to develop policy for

Ticket entry

What participants are agreeing to when they buy a ticket. The code of conduct exists but is not specific enough on political expression. What does it mean to come to an inclusive event, and what does that commit you to?

Flags

The most contested topic in the room. There is currently an unwritten norm against national flags — connected to the broader principle of leaving the default world behind — but it isn’t written down, isn’t enforced consistently, and breaks down entirely when specific geopolitical conflicts arrive on site. Several positions were argued: that no flags creates a level playing field and reduces the risk of tribalism; that banning flags is itself a political act that silences some people more than others; that the asymmetry matters (some flags carry associations of ongoing violence that others don’t). No resolution; this is exactly the kind of question the working group needs to work through. What was clear: leaving it unwritten is no longer viable.

Art pieces with political content

Where the line is between self-expression, which the event actively supports, and content that creates an unsafe or hostile environment for other participants. The existing decommodification principle (no brands, no default-world commercial presence) was offered as a partial analogy; the question is whether something similar applies to political symbols.

The broader tension

The session surfaced a genuine disagreement that won’t be resolved by policy alone: some people experience the event as a rare space to step outside geopolitical identity entirely, and feel that political symbols disrupt that; others experience their national or political identity as inseparable from who they are, and feel that being asked to leave it at the gate is itself a form of exclusion. Both positions were expressed with force and personal weight. The working group’s job is not to pick a side but to find a framework that leads on site can use when these situations arise, and that the community can vote on and stand behind.

What’s next

A working group is forming. If you’re interested, speak to Tilke or watch Discord for the channel opening. The first task is to check what Spanish law determines, then draft a proposal covering the three areas above. The proposal will go to the community for input before any vote.

How We’re Structured: Roles, Responsibilities & Reporting
⚡ All togetherSaturday 17 October

What this session was

The first of two sessions run by Céline and Anna Tam, based on a survey sent to leads, coordinators, and board members. The goal: translate survey data into concrete decisions on how departments are structured, how they report, and what coordinators and board members are responsible for.

Survey snapshot

26 respondents: 12 leads, 9 coordinators, 4 board members. Top themes from qualitative responses: distribute knowledge, make decisions visible to the community, protect departmental autonomy, and handle conflict fairly.

Decision 1: Coordinator structure per department

The proposal: Each department should have two people in the coordinator role to ensure knowledge transfer and continuity.

The discussion surfaced a genuine tension between two models: a named coordinator plus a deputy (clearer chain of contact, avoids the “everyone’s responsible so nobody is” problem), versus two equal co-coordinators (more autonomy, better for departments that naturally rotate responsibilities). Several people flagged that finding even one coordinator is hard for some departments, let alone two.

What cut through: the actual need is that someone always knows who to call, that reporting doesn’t fall through the cracks, and that knowledge doesn’t disappear when one person leaves. Whether that’s framed as co-coordinators or coordinator-plus-deputy is secondary.

Temperature of the room: Slight majority for one coordinator and one named deputy. Agreed by the ALM as the working model for 2027, with the understanding this will be revisited as the organisation matures.

Decision 2: Reporting frequency

The proposal: Departments report according to a jointly defined calendar rather than on a fixed universal schedule.

Discussion landed on the idea that reporting is driven by dependencies — power needs everyone’s power requirements by a certain date, production needs to know if build is on track — not by a top-down check-in rhythm. Daniela’s annual roadmap (Development → Pre-production → On-site → Post-event) provides the skeleton; departments feed into it and set milestones collaboratively.

The immediate tool is the Humans calendar, which already exists and is accessible to everyone. A dependency matrix (who needs what from whom and when) was flagged as a next step.

Agreed: Reporting frequency is defined per department according to the jointly developed event calendar. Not autonomous in isolation — shaped by what other departments need.

Decision 3: Coordinator role description

Presented as a synthesis of survey responses, not a top-down invention. Core responsibilities: department leadership, planning and delivery, communication and alignment (can be delegated), support and problem-solving within the team, risk escalation, people and knowledge management, budget oversight, and community transparency.

Key framing from the discussion: coordinators are responsible for ensuring these things happen — they are not required to do all of them personally. Delegation within the department is expected and encouraged. The role is about holding accountability, not centralising execution.

Agreed by show of hands.

Decision 4: Board role description

Presented alongside the coordinator scope. The board’s legal obligations as a Spanish association are fixed regardless of what the ALM decides — legal and financial liability, interface with authorities, GA convening. The role description captures what sits on top of that: governance, strategic direction (framed as “contribute to,” not “decide”), financial oversight, risk management, organisational support, and escalation.

Discussion flagged two concerns: the word “strategic direction” could be read as overreach; and the last two bullet points (“promote clear roles,” “participate in board-level decisions”) were felt to be too vague. Both were noted for refinement.

The group also clarified the fundamental check built into the association structure: board members serve at the pleasure of the asociados, who can call a general assembly and remove the board at any time. This is the structural limit on board power, not a role description.

Agreed by show of hands, with the vague bullet points flagged for tightening post-ALM.

  • Dependency matrix to be built alongside the Gantt chart as part of the 2027 roadmap
  • Board role description to be tightened on the vague bullet points

Board composition

Board selection is governed by the statutes and cannot be changed by the ALM — any amendment requires a GA vote with a two-thirds majority. Current structure: up to eight board members (president, vice president, secretary, treasurer, plus up to four non-executive members); two-year terms; maximum four terms.

On diversity, the discussion acknowledged that the first election happened under conditions of extreme uncertainty, which tended to attract a narrow profile of applicants. Agreed: A working group starting from Monday will develop concrete proposals for board election reform for the GA to vote on. Anyone interested is welcome to join.

Coordinator selection

Starting point from Part 1: roles to be advertised openly (Discord, newsletter, website), and a consent check to run before anyone takes up a coordinator role, managed by the consent response team on a case-by-case basis. The session then turned to the selection process when there are multiple candidates — which is where agreement broke down.

The core tension was between departmental autonomy (the department knows best who should coordinate it) and broader community accountability (coordinators affect the whole event, not just their department, and closed internal selection can calcify departments). Arguments made for each:

For community-level input: coordinators are accountable to the whole organisation, not just their team; election processes create a mandate and energise people; departments that self-select can become closed loops that are hard to change from outside.

For department-led selection: coordinators are almost never in competition — the real problem is finding anyone at all; interview-style processes feel corporate and put off people who aren’t naturally self-promotional; the people who know the work best are the ones who should be selecting; community votes reward visibility, not capability.

A concrete process proposal from the floor that drew interest: open the role publicly, invite anyone who feels like a stakeholder to a selection conversation (not a formal interview), prioritise inviting the outgoing coordinator and current department members, and use that conversation to check the candidate understands what they’re taking on rather than to rank them competitively. If only one person wants the role, they take it. If there are multiple, the group works out whether they can share responsibilities; if there’s a genuine conflict of vision, a decision is needed but the mechanism for that wasn’t resolved.

Not decided. The room agreed it wasn’t ready to vote on a specific process. The organisational structure working group is tasked with bringing back a concrete proposal, incorporating the range of views heard here.

Additional note added to the coordinator role description: coordinators should document problems they encountered and how they solved them, as part of handover — this feeds directly into the knowledge transfer goal agreed in Part 1.

Deselection and paid roles

Both topics were pitched briefly at the end of the session rather than discussed in full, given time constraints. No decisions were taken. These are carried to the working group and potentially a community call.

  • Organisational structure working group to develop coordinator selection process proposal

  • Deselection process and paid/stipended role transparency to be addressed in working group or community call

Werkhaus: From Volunteer Camp to Org Infrastructure
ParallelSaturday 17 October

What this session was

A working group to clarify the relationship between Werkhaus and the organisation — what the org pays for, what the barrio does for itself, where the roles sit, and how to prevent a repeat of this year’s near-miss on having no one step up to run it.

The core problem

Werkhaus is simultaneously integral to the event: it’s where the year-round, setup, strike and very involved volunteers live; and at the same time is a self-organising barrio with its own culture and funds. That dual nature creates blurry lines around budget, roles, and responsibility, and this year nearly tipped into a crisis when no one stepped up to be Werkhaus lead until Rory jumped in at the last minute.

What was agreed

The Werkhaus Lead becomes an official volunteer role. Rather than hoping someone from the camp steps up, the lead position will be listed as a named volunteer role in Humans with defined shifts, particularly covering the last four days of setup when the workload spikes. The role is still chosen from within the camp by the previous year’s members but it needs to be decided by ALM time so planning can start immediately after. If no one from camp steps up by a set deadline, the role opens externally.

The lead role splits into three sub-roles to distribute the workload that Rory was carrying alone: lead/overall coordination, treasurer, and sign-up management. Sign-up management in particular is its own significant job and shouldn’t sit on the same person as everything else.

Camp membership and size stays the same: 50 euros per person (with low-income allowance), max 70 members, with 5–10 spaces always held back for people who need to move camps during the event. Event size scaling can be reviewed if the overall event grows significantly.

A phased model for Werkhaus’s role during the event was agreed in principle. During setup and strike: Werkhaus is the crew’s home — it runs the coordinator corner, it’s the social hub, it’s where everything happens. In the third week (pre-event influx onwards): the coordinator corner transitions out of Werkhaus and into the Event Ops Hub. Werkhaus becomes the lounge, the crew’s off-duty space. This solves the long-standing tension between people arriving fresh and excited for the event walking into a space full of exhausted crew who just want to decompress.

Fluff distribution will move to the Event Ops Hub from pre-event week and during event time rather than running through Werkhaus. The hub will hold a freezer for ice and a shelf for fluff supplies, with a daily refill run from Werkhaus stock. This keeps the Werkhaus front-of-house clear of the volunteer-operations function during the event and stops non-camp members wandering into the fluff area.

A back bar / private hangout space for camp members during the event is on the table as a design goal for 2027, to be worked out through the site design process.

Budget and finances: the org continues to cover food, water, power, transport, containers, fluff, and ice for Werkhaus. The 50-euro camp fees stay in Werkhaus’s own pot (currently ~€3,700 in Hardcastle’s account) for camp-decided extras like the pool, speakers, bar stock, and similar. This autonomy is deliberate: it lets the camp make its own spending decisions outside of the community’s budget. A treasurer role within the pre-event Werkhaus leads structure will formalise this going forward.

Financial reporting to the org will improve — clearer tracking of what Werkhaus spends from org budget vs its own funds, especially now that the event hub model changes where fluff and ice costs sit.

  • Treasurer and sign-up manager to be identified from the camp
  • Back bar design to feed into the site design process
  • Final budget reconciliation from 2026 to inform 2027 planning

Event Identity
Open spaceSaturday 17 October

What this session was

An informal idea-gathering session to get a community temperature check on event identity and outreach: what kind of event do we want to be, what kind of people do we want to attract, and how do we communicate that. Not a decision session; a starting point for a working group.

Why this matters now

Borja needs a clearer sense of the event’s values and positioning before he can make content decisions. Without community alignment, every piece of content attracts fifty people saying “that’s not us.” The goal is enough shared understanding that comms can act with some confidence.

The session also connects to a concrete December deadline: per the 2027 roadmap, the event’s visual identity needs to be finalised before ticket sales open in January; covering the designed look and feel, assets for socials, website, wristbands, and other areas requiring graphic design. Alongside that, an outreach plan and content calendar need to be in place for launch, covering the ticket sale phases and key announcements.

What people said (round-robin)

The group went around and shared their instincts in roughly two-minute turns. The themes were consistent enough to feel like genuine alignment rather than politeness.

Tell the story, not the party

The event has an extraordinary story: people show up to nothing, build a temporary city, then leave nothing behind. That story, told well, attracts exactly the right people and repels the wrong ones. Content should centre the setup, the volunteer experience, the coordination, the co-creation; not the spectacle.

Co-creation as the core identity

The event is not a festival. It’s a temporary city built by the people who attend it. Every structure, every experience, every system is made by someone who chose to make it. That is what is unique and what should be communicated. Several people suggested that advertising volunteer roles and shifts —not the event itself— might be the most honest and effective way to reach the right people.

Sustainable, intentional growth — not mass scaling

General agreement that the event shouldn’t chase numbers. Fixed costs mean that some growth actually helps (more tickets = lower ticket price = more accessible). But the concern about losing what makes it special was universal. No one in the room wants a Burning Man trajectory. The question is how to grow without diluting.

The filter is a feature

Several people noted that this year, the lack of mainstream advertising meant that everyone who came had actively sought it out. That is a good filter. The wrong kind of outreach would break that. Harsh conditions, self-reliance, the commitment required: these should be communicated honestly, not softened, because they are what pre-qualify the right participants.

Word of mouth has limits

It’s powerful but keeps the community within existing bubbles: same age range, same demographics, same networks. If the goal includes reaching younger people, people without money, neurodivergent people looking for community, or people outside the current bubble, word of mouth alone won’t do it. New channels and new approaches are needed, but the question of which ones and how remains open.

The “repel and attract” principle

Content should be designed so that when the wrong person sees it, they think “that looks boring/hard/weird” — and when the right person sees it, they think “what the hell is this and why haven’t I heard of it before?” That’s the creative brief in one sentence.

What not to be

The session was clear that sex-positive positioning is not how the group wants the event communicated externally, whatever its internal culture. The event’s identity is closer to “builderburg” —known in the burn world for building an unusually large amount of stuff— and to radical co-creation, not hedonism.

A French academic study on a similar event (covering 2011–2015, 170 pages)

Was flagged as a useful resource: it documents that the event deliberately avoided advertising beyond one-to-one word of mouth, that smaller scale created stronger community bonds, and that the event’s identity is deliberately undefinable. The conclusion: don’t put the event at the centre of communications— put the culture it creates at the centre.

Ideas that came up for outreach

  • Stories and short-form interviews with crew and setup volunteers
  • Ambassador programme: give community members materials and encouragement to do outreach in their own networks
  • Communicating only the volunteer roles and shifts as the entry point
  • Local meetups and events (as has happened in Barcelona and Sariñena for similar events) to grow organically in adjacent communities
  • Content that travels through burn-adjacent networks and communities, not mainstream social
  • Younger/lower-income ticket tiers to address the financial access barrier

Next steps

This session was a starting point. A working group is needed to turn this into actual creative direction and a content plan. People interested in contributing —particularly on the visual identity side— should make themselves known to Borja and Daniela.

New Site: Purchase and Infrastructure
⚡ All togetherSunday 18 October

What this session was

Pablo and Ward presented the current state of the new site acquisition: where the process stands, what the financial model looks like, and what changes for each department if we move there. The session was part briefing, part open question to the teams: what would be different for your area, and what would it cost?

Where we are with the site

The new site is close to the current one, roughly three times the size of the yellow grid, with good road access and promising sound test results. Two owners hold the land. The working group is pursuing the portions needed for the event and exploring whether adjacent parcels —particularly the high-value farmland— can be sold off to a co-buyer rather than purchased outright, reducing the overall acquisition cost significantly.

The process is at feasibility stage. A permit consultation with INAGA and CHE has been submitted, but there’s been internal disruption on their end. A role became vacant, the new person was confused by existing correspondence, and it’s not yet clear whether the submission needs to be restarted. If it does, that triggers a fresh three-month window in which no response means no. December remains the target for clarity. A permit allowing all requested dates has been submitted; the working group is also pursuing the case that a June pre-event presence is essentially the same as what the organisation already does at the current site each year.

The financial model

Pablo walked through a financial model built around a purchase price in the region of €700k (with ~10% in transaction costs on top), paid over three years to the owners rather than in a lump sum. That staged payment effectively provides free financing.

Community loans are the proposed funding mechanism, not governance stakes. Based on prior surveys, around 300 people have indicated they’d lend an average of ~€3,000 each at 2% interest, repaid over up to ten years. Lenders would have no governance rights, only the right to recover their money. Default protections still need to be defined.

The model also captures operational savings from owning rather than renting: this year the organisation paid €32k in site rent. On top of that, Pablo ran estimates for toilets (~€33k/year rental and servicing reduced to ~€25k/year once permanent infrastructure is built, after an upfront investment of ~€100k), containers, water, and production infrastructure. If all the improvements land as modelled, the projected saving is around €192k per year — which would dramatically accelerate loan repayment. Peter cautioned clearly that these are optimistic-case projections, and that real-world timelines, volunteer capacity, and cost overruns will stretch the payback horizon. A 15-year structure with early repayment options is more realistic than a five-year one.

One important caveat raised: in year one on a new site, almost none of those savings materialise. Grid connection, permanent toilets, and on-site container storage are all medium-to-long-term projects. The first year (or two) would still run largely on the same rental and servicing costs as the current site, just with the added complexity of an unfamiliar location.

What changes for each department

Production & Logistics · Vendors are already briefed and can change an address. The bigger cost is the pre-event work: road-building, land clearance, site visits with power, infra, and city planning teams, potentially paid contractors for machinery. The risk assessment document (19 risk categories, ~€2–2.5k to produce, ~€600/year to update) would need to be redone for the new site, with two additional categories for flooding. Daniela proposed creating a hot-season and cold-season version that can be updated annually rather than rebuilt each time.

Infrastructure & City Planning · The new site has no pre-prepared land, no Ayuntamiento-scraped ground, no existing road layout. Water drainage mapping, slope management, and road planning all start from scratch. Modern GPS and mapping tools help, but this is a significant uplift. Ward and Daniela have already walked the two proposed access roads with local authorities, and there are a couple of vendors who could do the work. Topographical mapping needs to happen early and comprehensively — water placement, camp layout, and emergency access all depend on it.

Power · Grid connection is a long-term project with large upfront costs (cabling and distribution infrastructure are expensive). Year one will run on generators regardless. The conversation about solar storage, battery infrastructure, and eventual grid connection is worth starting now but can’t be costed meaningfully yet. The event’s installed capacity this year was 300kW peak; the available grid connection at the new site is ~108kW, which may be sufficient given that peak installed ≠ peak used — but this needs proper technical scoping.

Barrios Support · A new site map means reorienting participants who’ve just learned this one. No immediate benefit from renting for a year. The longer-term upside is significant though: on-site container storage could reduce barrio costs enough to open the door to smaller, cheaper, more experimental barrios — which could also attract a different attendee demographic and increase overall event income.

Toilets · Compost toilets are not legally possible until the land is owned. The blocker is waste disposal: no company in the region has the permits to handle compost toilet waste as currently structured. A reed-bed filtration project (similar to one the local Comarca mayor runs for a village) has been explored, with contacts passed on for follow-up. Servicing remains required for any interim period. All permanent toilet infrastructure is ownership-gated.

Cantina · Broadly the same as current site. Key concern: cantina must not be placed in a flood-risk zone.

Participant Wellness / Malfare · Emergency access road placement is the critical variable. A cooler site (if the date shift to June happens) could reduce heat-related cases, partially offsetting the added complexity of a new location.

Radios · The site’s topography — hills and cliff faces — may interfere with radio coverage in ways the current flat site doesn’t. Relay antennas may be needed. There’s an existing radio tower on the land that could be assessed for reuse.

NoInfo & Participant-Facing Roles · A new site means a lost population for the first year. Everyone from NoInfo to site leads to gate will spend significant energy helping people who simply don’t know where anything is.

Key questions raised

Governance of large loans: if one lender provides a disproportionate share of the financing, how do you protect community governance without making their loan unattractive? Pablo’s position: loans carry no governance rights, only repayment rights, with defined default protections.

One question raised was whether the 2027 event could happen on the new site under a rental agreement before any purchase is finalised. The broad consensus was that the amount of upfront work involved (land prep, roads, drainage, permits) only makes sense if the organisation is on a path to ownership. A straight rental with no purchase option would mean absorbing all of that effort and cost for a site we don’t end up owning. So if a rental is the first step, it needs to be structured as a lease-to-buy: a contract that locks in the option to purchase.

Daniel (Board) raised a structural concern that applies regardless: trying to plan and deliver an event while simultaneously managing a site acquisition could be a recipe for both failing. His proposal —which the group broadly agreed with— is to keep the acquisition as a separate, self-contained workstream until a clear go/no-go decision is made. December is the decision month. Once the direction is confirmed, the community can mobilise accordingly.

What’s still open / post-ALM actions

  • Co-buyer / plot-split negotiation with both owners
  • Financial model sense-check against real 2026 cost data
  • Legal and financing structure (community loan mechanics, default protections, entity format)
  • December go/no-go decision required on whether 2027 event moves to new site (rent or purchase) or stays at current site with new dates
  • Upon knowledge of purchase:
    • Compost toilet feasibility: contact the engineers who designed the Comarca reed-bed project, and reactivate working group
    • Power scoping: working group to assess grid connection, battery storage, and interim generator strategy
    • Topographical survey and drainage mapping of new site

The Werkhaus Lead is now an official volunteer role listed in Humans, with defined shifts mainly covering the last four days of pre-event week (at the same time as barrios arrivals). The role splits into three year-round: lead, treasurer, and sign-up manager. If no one from camp steps up by a set deadline, it opens externally. Volunteer fluff moves to the Event Ops Hub from pre-event week. The phased model agreed in principle: Werkhaus as crew operational hub during setup and strike; crew lounge during the event. Budget and membership structure stays the same as 2026.

Families Inclusion

Working group: Families

The session quickly identified that the main blocker is a lack of information: a similar event used to have more families, and nobody’s sure why they left. Three research threads to follow up: talking to families who are coming, reaching out to those who don’t, and looking at what other burns with strong family infrastructure are doing. Chris to start with the former families lead, who now has kids and no longer attends. No structural decisions yet; this is an information-gathering phase.

New Site: Purchase and Infrastructure

Working group: ElSitio

Pablo walked through the financial model and the current state of the acquisition. Roughly three times the size of the current site, close by, with good road access and promising sound results. December is the decision month: go/no-go on whether 2027 moves to the new site or stays at the current one with a new date. The working group continues as a separate stream from event planning. Daniel’s point, broadly agreed: trying to do both at once risks both failing. If we move, the upfront work is significant regardless of whether we rent or buy, which is why any rental agreement needs to lock in a purchase option rather than leaving us with a year of hard work on land we don’t end up owning.

Closing reflections

The closing round was open; one minute each, no prompt beyond what people wanted to take home. A framework someone offered early on: one thing you liked, one thing you didn’t, one thing that’s still itching.

What came back, across many voices: genuine pride in what the event pulled off this year. Several people said this weekend felt more structured than they’d expected, with more new faces, and more energy moving in the right direction. First-timers said they’d arrived nervous and left feeling rooted. People who’d been sceptical about whether the ALM format would work said it had.

A few recurring itches: how to keep institutional knowledge alive as people move on; how to include people who couldn’t be in the room; how to grow without burning through successive waves of volunteers. The observation that there were very few people attending online was noted: better hybrid equipment and publishing the programme further in advance were suggested as fixes.

One practical note from Radios: a survey is going out to coordinators and departments before the end of the year on radio needs — how many radios, which channels — to feed into the Event Ops Hub design and help departments move toward more communication autonomy.

One invitation from Consent: the consent response work happens year-round, not just on site. If you want to be involved, talk to them.

The ALM feedback survey goes out soon.

Ticketing
Open spaceSunday 18 October

What this session was

An informal, open-floor input session on ticketing: how the organisation raises money from the community, how the low-income system works, and what could be done better. No decisions were made. The output feeds the Finance Working Group, which starts Monday.

Low-income tickets

This dominated the first half of the session, with several people sharing personal experience of the system. The consistent themes were:

The low-income price should keep pace with the full ticket price

The low-income price should be set in proportion to the full ticket price, and reviewed whenever the full price changes. Several people said they’d have been willing to pay more than the low-income price, but had no way to do so, and no mechanism to top up or gift the difference to someone who needed it more.

The number of low-income tickets available this year was lower than intended

A direct consequence of the compressed timeline and the organisation having very little cash early in the year. The intention going forward is to make the window longer and the allocation larger.

Low-income tickets are non-transferable (by design, since they’re discounted), but not currently refundable

This leaves people who bought one and can’t attend out of pocket. The intention going forward is to make them refundable via a queue system: anyone wanting a refund joins the queue, and the next ticket sold clears the next person in it. A workaround was also suggested: allowing the holder to pass a purchase link to someone else so they can buy at the same price, without the ticket itself transferring.

Two specific gaps were raised

Artists who receive art grants but still can’t afford a full ticket, and late-committing infrastructure crew who miss the deadline entirely due to work commitments. A proposal for both: ring-fence a small block of low-income codes (five to ten) available past the deadline, unused if not needed, for exactly these cases. A version of this exists for artists in similar events and could be activated.

The application process itself

A questionnaire rather than a self-declaration, was broadly valued. The concern is not the process but the allocation: the feeling that genuinely low-income people, including people living in vans and working setup and strike, are being outcompeted by the point system rather than being prioritised.

A separate idea floated

A community co-funded ticket specifically for people who’ve burnt out and drifted away. A way to bring them back while signalling that the community noticed their contribution and wants them there.

The broader system

The underlying challenge: most of the constraints on how the organisation sells tickets come from uncertainty. The wave system, early revenue pressure, and the difficulty of designing a generous low-income allocation all flow from not knowing whether the event will sell out. If the event reliably sells out at whatever capacity is set, the organisation can design any system it wants: calm allocation of low-income blocks, flexible waves, whatever makes sense; because the revenue is predictable.

The proposed refund system addresses part of this: rather than people trading tickets informally on Discord, anyone who can’t attend returns their ticket to a pool, and the next buyer clears the refund. This encourages early purchase (since you’re not locked in) while front-loading revenue for the organisation. The pool closes about a week before the event.

Vendor tickets

The question of vendors who become or want to become part of the community: attending specific barrios, gifting work, participating in the culture beyond their commercial role. This year, local-resident codes were made available to them but most cases have been handled ad hoc. The ask is to standardise this properly, for two reasons: legal (the organisation needs to know who is on site) and fairness (some vendors can get tickets through informal channels that others didn’t have access to). A clear, consistent process needs to be defined for 2027.

Comms and outreach

The session drifted into a broader conversation about how the event reaches new people: which channels to use, what kind of identity to project, and who makes those calls. The rough consensus: the comms team should have autonomy to experiment with platforms and approaches, guided by the identity work from the previous day’s session, without needing permission for each channel. Anyone who wants to post on a platform should be able to join the comms team and do it; anyone who doesn’t want to be on a platform doesn’t have to be, and shouldn’t have a veto over others who do.

What’s next

The Finance Working Group starts Monday at 6pm, meeting weekly. Ticketing system design, low-income allocation, and the refund mechanism are all on the agenda. If you want a say in how this works, join.
