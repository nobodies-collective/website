Survey results · Input to the 17 September vote

Event Dates 2027: what the survey says

948 people told us which weeks in 2027 they could come to Elsewhere, and in what capacity. This page sets out what they said — week by week, group by group, and in their own words — and links the full anonymised data so anyone can check the reading. It draws no conclusion: that is for the members’ vote.

Responses: 948, collected 23 August – 4 September 2026 Published: 5 September 2026 Companion page: What we know so far

The survey informs the vote; it is not the vote. Only association members vote on 17 September, using a ranked method. This survey was open to anyone, was shared through the newsletter, Discord, barrio channels and social media, and 447 of the 948 responses are anonymous — so it measures who answered, not the whole community. Read the caveats in the method section before quoting a number.

How to read the charts. Every week-by-week chart shows two dots per week: identified respondents (who answered through a personal link on Humans — ticket holders and volunteers, one response per account) and anonymous respondents (who used the public link). The label is the share of everyone in that group. The two populations do not always agree, and where they part is itself part of the result.

1. Who answered

Respondents could tick more than one description of themselves. 878 (93%) want to come in 2027; 508 build for their barrio, 146 build for the event (2+ weeks), 208 do strike, and 447 go to other burns. 328 (35%) ticked no build or strike role at all. By interface language: English 578, French 185, Spanish 90, German 56, Catalan 24, Italian 15.

477 responses came through a personal link on Humans (identified), 447 through the public link (anonymous), and 24 were completion-tracked only (counted in totals, in neither dot). The two populations differ in composition: event builders are mostly identified (92 vs 51), while people who go to other burns are more often anonymous (231 vs 205). Responses arrived in two waves: the launch on 23 August, and a second push on 31 August.

5. How flexible people are

72 respondents (8%) said they could come in any of the 16 weeks; 56 could manage two or fewer. The median respondent could make 8 of the 16.

Number of weeks each respondent could come (out of 16)

The people with the least room to move are the ones a change affects most. 40 respondents can only come in the late-June-to-July window — 17 of them only in the current week itself. Against that, 12 can only come in September and 8 only in late May–mid June. Looking at the two main alternative windows together: 597 people could make both a June and a September week, 149 June but not September, 136 September but not June, and 66 neither.

Reading the comments as a whole

The sharpest disagreement is not about moving; it is about what the heat is. For one group it is the point of the event — the reason you can live in the desert without clothes, the filter that keeps the place for people who really want it. For another it has become a safety problem — heatstroke, medical evacuations, physical build work at 40 °C. Both groups cite the same 2026 event as their evidence.

The second real tension is structural and nobody in it is being unreasonable: teachers, students and academics are locked to the summer break and say so plainly (a cluster of French teachers wrote near-identical short comments to that effect), while freelancers and people in tourism, events and hospitality say summer is their income season and they need the event out of it. Those two groups cannot both be given what they ask for by choosing a week.

Several long comments argue that the survey asks the wrong question. One lays out the Aragon regulation in detail — that an evacuation is triggered by an alert level declared by the regional authority, with no local discretion, and that it has already fired once — reframing “we might be evacuated” from a mood into a mechanism. A smaller group argue that the fix is a different site, not a different week. And one point of critique bears directly on the availability numbers: cannot come was the only way to say will not come. Some respondents who could attend in July but no longer want to will have ticked July as available, so the grid probably understates reluctance about the current dates rather than overstating it.

7. Method and caveats

  • Self-selected sample. The survey went out through the newsletter, Discord, barrio channels and social media. People who feel strongly about dates were more likely to answer. Treat the shares as the views of 948 engaged people, not of the whole community.
  • Identified vs anonymous. Identified responses came through a personal Humans link, one per account. Anonymous responses came through the public link and cannot be de-duplicated; at least one respondent wrote that they had answered twice. Neither population is “the real one”; the page shows both.
  • “Could come” means at least one part ticked. 78 grid cells (out of 15,168) had both Cannot come and a part ticked; those are counted as could come. No respondent left the grid blank.
  • Language is interface language, not nationality or residence.
  • Comments were tagged by a language model reading every comment in full; the theme counts are indicative, and every quote was checked against the source text and is verbatim (elisions marked with […]).
  • Anonymisation. Account IDs and usernames were removed from every response. In the comments, names, an email address, barrio and crew names attached to a role, employer names and other details that could point to one person were replaced with generic wording (16 of 378 comments). Nothing else was edited.

The complete anonymised responses, so anyone can check this page or run their own analysis. The JSON is the survey export with identifiers removed; the CSV is the same data flattened to one row per response.
