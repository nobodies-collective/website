ALM Session — Getting Into and Out of Roles (Part 2)

Date: Saturday 17 October 2026 (~2 hours including a break, in person, with some participants online)
With: Céline S (Organisational Structure working group, facilitating), Anna T (Organisational Structure working group; consent response lead), Daniel T (president), Peter D (treasurer), Chris O, Daniela (Production), Ant H, Joao T, Tilke W, Ellen K, Michael M (online), and other ALM participants A plenary session at the Annual Leadership Meeting (ALM, 15–19 October 2026), the second of two governance sessions prepared by the Organisational Structure working group from its survey of leads, coordinators and board members. The ALM is a space for discussion and for taking the temperature of the room; it cannot change the association’s statutes, which only the General Assembly can amend. Proposals recorded below are proposals, not decisions, unless stated otherwise.

This session followed Part 1 (roles, responsibilities and reporting) and covered how people get into and out of roles: board composition, the advertising of roles and a consent check for coordinators, the coordinator selection process, and, as short pitches after a break, deselection and paid roles. The room agreed to advertise all roles openly and to a consent check in principle. Coordinator selection was discussed at length but not decided. Board election reform, coordinator selection, deselection and paid roles were all carried forward to working groups. The organisers’ summary is on the ALM 2026 outcomes page.

1. Opening

  • Participants were asked to be aware of how much room they take up, so that people who speak less, or who feel less able to, have space to contribute. Everyone was asked to use the agreed facilitation signs.
  • Chris O said that he and Ellen K are happy to act as a sounding board for anyone who does not feel confident speaking up or making an argument in the room.

2. Board composition

  • The statutes govern. Céline S opened by noting that board selection is defined in the statutes and that this session is not the General Assembly. Board members are elected for two-year terms and can serve a maximum of four terms. Any change would come later as a proposal for the General Assembly to vote on.
  • Board size. Céline S asked whether the board should stay at its current eight members. Now that the board has been defined as strategic rather than operational, she felt that expanding it probably does not make sense. Chris O confirmed that this meant continuing with the current size; he noted that the statutes would allow more non-executive members. Daniel T said the room could agree on this socially, but could not decide it: the statutes stay as they are, and the board’s membership changes through people applying and being elected.
  • Staggered terms. A participant asked why terms are set at two years. Anna T noted the concern that it is not secure for the whole board to change at the same time, and that there is a wish to discuss amending this. As a statute change, it was left for a later discussion, once there are complete proposals.
  • A working group on board elections. Daniel T said the board has generally been open to statute changes that make elections more inclusive, such as how elections work and the quorum requirements. It had waited for the work on what board members and coordinators are for, which is now in a good place. He suggested a working group, starting in the following days, to propose how elections could become more dynamic, and said he would join it. Peter D added that such a working group can make a proposal, and that changing the statutes requires a two-thirds majority of the General Assembly. He also noted that members can request an extraordinary General Assembly, and that the General Assembly can remove board members, so a change does not have to wait for the end of a term.
  • Board diversity. Tilke W said it would be good for the board to be more diverse, and that the barrier is the selection process. Daniel T agreed that everyone shares this aim. He said the first election took place under conditions of extreme uncertainty: the association did not yet exist, nothing was set up, and the workload was unknown. Roles defined like that tend to attract a narrower range of applicants. Now that the association is established, with a bank account and rules in place, the role can be made much clearer and more appealing. Tilke W said that, from her conversations with people in less privileged positions, the main reason they had not applied was time and energy, not risk. She suggested looking for ways in which people with less time could still contribute.
  • Time commitment. Peter D said that, for anyone joining the board, time is the main requirement: being able to attend meetings, around two to three hours a week. When too few board members attend, the board cannot vote or move forward. Another participant said that serving on a board is a responsibility, and that having time to read, attend and engage is a basic requirement. Daniela said she understood the point about privilege to be that people in more comfortable circumstances tend to have more time. That was a valid conversation, but a separate one from this session.
  • Anna T noted that board diversity and time requirements were not the topic of this session. Chris O invited anyone interested to continue the conversation over dinner and then take it into a working group.

3. Coordinator roles: open advertising and a consent check

  • Advertising all roles. Anna T reported a clear message from the questionnaires: all roles (leads, coordinators and board) should be advertised whenever they come up, through several channels, such as a dedicated Discord channel, the newsletter and a page on the website. The aim is not to block a quick appointment when a role must be filled close to the event, but to avoid roles being handed from one person to a friend without the community being able to put themselves forward. No one objected; this was agreed.
  • A consent check. Anna T proposed a consent check before someone takes up a coordinator role. People organising within the association, and managing volunteers, would be held to a somewhat different standard from people attending the event. The details still need to be defined. The room agreed to this in principle, with the assessment delegated to the consent response team, case by case.
  • Questions raised. Joao T asked what would actually prevent someone from taking a role, and on what criteria. Anna T said that the consent processes are published on the website, while the details of individual cases are confidential, and that decisions are never made by one person alone and can be appealed. Chris O raised two concerns. First, if a candidate quietly drops out, others may assume the consent team said no; Anna T agreed this should be handled discreetly and thought a way could be found. Second, he asked what checks and balances the rest of the organisation has on the consent team’s process, to make sure it cannot be misused.
  • Michael M suggested that the check should not imply that a past consent issue is the only reason someone might not be suitable. There should be a way to record other reasons too. Anna T suggested this might be handled in Humans. She added that the consent team must not become a bottleneck: checking every volunteer at sign-up had proved unworkable. Peter D noted that there are only some twenty-five to thirty coordinator roles.
  • Céline S took these as feedback on the process, and suggested more transparency about how the consent process works, possibly at a community call.

4. Coordinator selection

The session then turned to how coordinators are selected, especially when more than one person wants a role. Many views were heard.

  • Starting proposal. Céline S presented a draft, “really just a proposal”. The role would be advertised openly. The department, which should keep its autonomy, would propose one or more candidates, and further applications would be invited. A single candidate with no other applicants would take the role, subject to the consent check. If there were several candidates and the department could not decide, there would be a General Assembly vote.
  • A council of coordinators and board. Chris O did not think a General Assembly vote for each coordinator is practical, or the right level for an operational role. He proposed that the board and the coordinators together form a coordinators’ council, which would approve new departments and their coordinators.
  • Sharing the work rather than competing. Ellen K said the usual problem is finding anyone at all, not too many candidates. When there are several, they could sit down together and divide the work according to what each is good at, with a separate mechanism only for real conflicts. She also felt that a one- to two-week application period is long, given how short the deadlines are. Another participant agreed: if several people want to be involved, the department should use them, or point them to other departments that need similar skills.
  • Open applications, and conflicting visions. Daniel T said anyone outside the department should be able to put themselves forward; otherwise there is little point advertising openly. He said the case the draft does not cover is when candidates want to take the department in different directions. In that case they cannot simply work together, and a decision between them is needed; that is what a selection process is for. A participant agreed that anyone should be able to apply. If several did, they could be voted on by the coordinators and board members rather than the General Assembly. Daniel T noted that a members’ vote need not be a formal General Assembly.
  • A small selection panel. Daniela said roles should be announced everywhere at once. She described a model in which candidates talk with a small group, for example two people from the department and two other coordinators (one or two board members were also mentioned), rather than a standing council. Where candidates have different visions, they could present proposals, and the department might be best placed to choose.
  • Experience from other organisations.
    • One participant described another regional burn. There, every role has a published job description, even when filled. Applications go first through the consent check and then to the relevant coordinators and board. If there are several applicants, there is a conversation. They did not think a vote would help: it is a job, not a popularity contest, and it is rarely competitive.
    • Another participant described a cooperative. Candidates for its general coordination are confidential, and the board selects, because the board is accountable.
    • A third suggested an endorsement route. Someone already known in the organisation could take a role if endorsed by the department and/or coordinators and board members; others would have an interview.
  • Asked whether the same would apply to coordinator deputies, Daniela said it probably would. She also clarified that the role being filled is the coordinator role; delegating particular tasks within a team is up to the coordinators.
  • Community accountability. Daniel T said that a purely internal process, where a department selects its own coordinator from among its members, tends to be static and lacks community accountability. A department can keep expanding its own activity even when the wider community does not want that, and outsiders find it hard to join or change it. An open process with input from the community would give a coordinator a mandate for change. It can also be energising, because candidates think about what they stand for. This could be a members’ vote rather than a full General Assembly.
  • Keeping it organic. A participant was concerned that the association is drifting towards a company-style system. Many people would not feel comfortable sending a CV, writing a letter or being interviewed by board members; they wanted the process to stay organic. Another participant clarified that, in the model they had described, the interview is about explaining the role and checking the person understands what they are taking on, not testing them.
  • Being chosen. Chris O said accountability is fundamental. If some people do not feel able to take on roles, that is a cultural issue to work on. Day to day, a coordinator is accountable to their team and the other coordinators. Being chosen through a process, rather than just raising a hand, gives the role value, energy and purpose. He agreed with much of what Daniel T said, though he did not think selection needs to go as wide as the whole membership.
  • Experience and responsibility. Michael M said that teams are more often undersubscribed than oversubscribed, and that the people working in a team should be involved in meeting candidates. Coordinator roles can cause real harm to the event if done badly. Those with experience, and the production coordinator, who carries business responsibilities such as dealing with contractors, should have some say. He would trust their judgement over a popularity contest.
  • Department knowledge and budget. Ant H said the department’s knowledge needs to be valued alongside wider input. Technical coordinator roles usually go to people who have already worked in the team for a year or two. Teams should be as autonomous as possible. The community’s control comes mainly through the budget and through agreements about what a department will deliver.
  • Another participant shared reservations about candidates presenting themselves to the General Assembly: the skills needed for these roles are not the same as those for self-promotion. They also asked whether people should be able to stay in a role year on year. That was not answered in this session.
  • Anna T summed up that a coordinator’s department, the other coordinators, the board and the community are all stakeholders, and that no system yet meets all of their needs.
  • A process proposal from the floor. A participant proposed the following process. The role is announced to everyone, with a role description. Applicants are invited to a meeting open to anyone who feels like a stakeholder, with the department and current coordinators specifically invited, and the outgoing coordinator especially. The meeting is a conversation to check that the candidate understands what they are taking on, not a ranking. With several candidates, it would usually become clear who is ready to step up and who would rather join the team. There need not be a fixed group of decision-makers. Céline S welcomed it as a concrete proposal for the working group.
  • No vote taken. Céline S proposed an indicative vote between three options: a centrally defined selection process for all departments, each department defining its own process, or not ready to decide. Participants pointed out that the options were not mutually exclusive: a central process could, for example, leave interviews to each department. There was also disagreement about which option the proposal from the floor fell under. The room concluded it was not ready, and no vote was held. Céline S invited everyone with strong views or proposals to join the Organisational Structure working group.
  • Lessons learned. A participant proposed adding to the coordinator role description a short record of the problems met in the role and how they were solved, for the next coordinator. Céline S suggested including it under reporting; this was taken on.

5. Deselection (pitch)

Given the time, the room agreed to take a break and then hear short pitches on the two remaining topics, without discussion.

  • Anna T presented the working group’s thinking so far. A deselection process will probably not be used often, but if it exists, then when it is needed it can be caring. The aim is to avoid people being suddenly removed from a role with no process and no say. Good processes earlier on may also make deselection unnecessary.
  • She listed four reasons someone might be asked to step out of a role, ideally into another one: inactivity; concern about how the role is being carried out; interpersonal conflict, which can lead others in a team to leave; and a concern report, which would be delegated to the consent response team.
  • What counts as inactivity depends on the role and the time of year. It should be made clear to people when they take on a role that, if they stop responding, someone else may need to step in.
  • She is working on mediation processes within the organisation. These include an on-call mediation and facilitation team drawn from the many facilitators in the community, and a supported way for people to raise concerns about how something is being handled. These processes would come first, before any deselection. Who makes a deselection decision, and how, is still to be worked out.

6. Paid roles (pitch)

  • Daniela presented a proposal to break down the work she currently covers into defined roles, as had been asked of her in person and through the surveys:
    • Overall project management: the event’s whole timeline, from the first planning to the final feedback, invoicing and tax filing, with an overview of every department’s and supplier’s timeline.
    • Production and logistics: currently combined, but ideally two people, one planning and one executing.
    • Finance: bookkeeping, checking invoices, quarterly filings, card and purchase administration, and keeping track of each department’s budget. Outsourcing this to an external accountant had been considered as cheaper than a full-time role, but internal accountability and traceability are reasons a finance role is still needed.
    • Year-round people coordination: supporting coordinators, leads and volunteers throughout the year, and, for anyone working for the association, contracts, invoices, legal compliance and payments.
    • Communications: the side of comms that affects ticket sales and outreach, and the association’s image and crisis communication, as distinct from managing community channels.
    • On-site coordination: a role hired for specific weeks, as distinct from year-round coordinators. Two people minimum, three ideally, in each phase (first crew, setup, pre-event, event, strike and site return). No one should do more than two weeks in a row, given the workload. She proposed this be paid because it is hourly, on-call work that handles incidents, emergencies, permit-related matters and legal issues on site.
    • Paid work for site preparation and strike: the first and last weeks are the most labour-intensive. Few people can take three to five weeks off unpaid, and these weeks are the hardest to recruit for. She acknowledged that there are valid arguments about how this fits the association’s principles, and asked everyone to think about it and look at how similar events handle it.
  • Ant H strongly supported a paid labour pool for build and strike. Some people in the community are “time rich and money poor”. The association already pays companies a great deal, and some long-standing volunteers with the right skills could benefit from paid work.
  • As agreed, there was no discussion; questions were left for informal conversations and for the working group.

Outcomes

  • Board composition is governed by the statutes and was not changed. Any amendment needs a two-thirds majority of the General Assembly. Staggered terms and board diversity were raised and left for a working group.
  • Agreed: all roles (leads, coordinators and board) are to be advertised openly through several channels.
  • Agreed in principle: a consent check before anyone takes up a coordinator role, delegated to the consent response team case by case. The process still needs to be defined, including how it stays discreet and accountable.
  • Coordinator selection: not decided. The views heard ranged from department-led selection to community-level input. They are carried to the Organisational Structure working group, which is to bring back a concrete proposal.
  • The coordinator role description is to include a lessons-learned / handover record under reporting.
  • Deselection and paid roles were presented as pitches only; no decisions were taken.

Next steps

  • A working group on board election reform, open to anyone, is to start after the ALM and develop proposals for the General Assembly to vote on.
  • The Organisational Structure working group is to develop a proposal for the coordinator selection process. People with proposals or strong views are invited to join.
  • Deselection and paid roles are to be taken forward in the working group and possibly at a community call.
  • The consent check process is to be defined, with more transparency about how the consent process works (suggested for a community call).
