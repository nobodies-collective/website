Sound Policy Working Group — conversation with a yellow-zone camp — Transcript

Date: 20 November 2026

Edited transcript prepared by the working group. Filler, repetitions, audio troubleshooting, greetings and unrelated personal conversation have been removed. People, camps and art projects have been anonymized. Speaker labels follow the apparent turns in the unlabelled source: Speaker A describes their camp’s experience; Speaker B discusses the guide revision. This is a lightly condensed edited transcript, not a verbatim record. Proposed changes and participants’ interpretations are not presented as adopted rules. Technical suggestions are retained as discussion, not verified acoustic advice. Published in English only.

Current practice and the camp’s experience

Speaker A: We believe we are following the current Yellow Zone policy. We have three decibel meters and check that we aren’t crossing the 85 dB limit. We generally don’t play music after 04:00. There was one party that went a little later, but never after 05:00, and we don’t want to play until 07:00. We wouldn’t mind a slightly lower volume or an earlier finish.
I think City Planning is part of why this has become a problem. Our plaza was crowded. This year we received the space we had requested and realized we had apparently received more than that in previous years. We didn’t have enough room for our structures, but our neighbours helped by shifting their layout.
Our system has around six speakers and a subwoofer. There isn’t currently a limiter, but we wouldn’t object to one. Inside the dome, the speakers face towards the middle, which lets us lower the volume and focus sound inside. The covering is still fabric, so I can imagine there is sound leakage.
For the parties we held on the playa, we pointed the speakers towards the middle of the playa, rather than towards the camps in front of us. There could still have been leakage, and I don’t know how far it travelled.
Speaker B: One existing rule that hasn’t been communicated well in recent years is that speakers should face outward, towards deep playa, rather than towards the centre. I also encountered that communication problem in a previous camp.
In your dome, tilting speakers down towards the dance floor could help contain sound. We could also look at mitigation using the position of heavy objects such as containers near the system.
Speaker A: We’re willing to do those things, and we already do some of them. Including practical guidance in the sound guide is no problem for us.

Yellow, orange and different expectations

Speaker B: My conversations suggest Yellow may be too permissive or unclear. Blue protects quiet; Green is more oriented towards daytime workshops and people who party elsewhere. Yellow is a middle ground, but people expect different things from it.
One possibility is to reduce Yellow’s area, enlarge Orange and reduce Yellow’s late-night allowance. Some people want Yellow to focus on workshops and small daytime parties.
Speaker A: Why wouldn’t those people choose Green?
Speaker B: Green may be too restrictive for them. Another way to look at this is whether your location could become Orange, perhaps with a small move, rather than expecting your camp to change into something it isn’t.
Speaker A: We’re already beside Orange. Depending on the eventual rules, we wouldn’t mind being in Orange, but we’re definitely not a Red camp. Changing our colour alone won’t solve the problem. It might permit more noise than we actually want to make, while the neighbours who are already disturbed would still be there.
Speaker B: We’re also discussing designated Red perimeter locations for large stationary sound installations and loud art-car parties, facing outward. Red might need some adjustment to accommodate that, with Orange extending accordingly.
We could keep Red’s role and lower the allowances in Orange and Yellow, so the largest parties belong in Red. Orange could describe camps that want night parties without operating as huge clubs or playing constantly.
Alongside the dB limits and hours, we should explain the character and purpose of each colour so people can choose more knowingly.
Speaker A: Perception matters as well as measurements. Lowering decibels doesn’t necessarily remove annoyance. Putting camps that want quiet nights right beside camps that want to party can fail even when the sound fits the policy. Both have a place at the event, but perhaps not next to each other.
A short description of each zone’s atmosphere could help, alongside some rearrangement and practical mitigation. One group seems to want the greener side of Yellow, while we want its more Orange side. Yellow contains camps that interpret the same policy very differently.
I don’t mind where the boundary ends up, or whether another distinction is needed. We’d like to continue our activities and adapt to our neighbours’ needs. Reorienting speakers or placing a container differently are things we can consider.

Boundaries, sleeping areas and practical mitigation

Speaker B: Even with clearer zones, the boundaries will remain potential conflict areas: Orange can spill into Yellow. Moving incompatible neighbours further apart can help. During placement and camp design, we could also consider where trucks or containers might help reduce spill towards quieter areas.
We discussed speaker direction, nearby heavy objects and raising subwoofers as possible mitigation methods.

[Editorial note: Claims about containers, subwoofer elevation and bass transmission were informal technical suggestions. Their effectiveness depends on the setup and would need checking before inclusion as technical guidance.]

Speaker A: The current policy says speakers should face outward and sleeping tents shouldn’t be in that direction. But there has been freecamping behind our camp, and our own tents are also at the back, as they are in many camps. An absolute rule against sleeping tents in that line would create a City Planning problem.
Speaker B: We’re moving towards a guide that needs to be applied sensibly. Outward-facing speakers are the general direction, but an inward-facing four-point setup aimed down into a dome might contain sound better than a large system simply pointed outward.
The intention is to avoid aiming directly at neighbouring sleeping areas, rather than impose an impossible layout. We’ve had neighbouring camps accidentally aim systems at each other’s tents. Communication and a small change in direction could avoid that.
The placement map could show general arrows before camps design their layout. Those would help people orient themselves. The aim is to prevent problems and avoid spilling sound onto our friends.
Your playa-facing party looked good, but an open dance floor is harder to contain. It may fit Orange better than Yellow, where it can affect quieter artworks and neighbouring camps.
Speaker A: We had also considered putting speakers further out on the playa and pointing them back towards our camp.
Speaker B: That is another arrangement to investigate. Tilting speakers towards the audience rather than projecting over them can help. We discussed modest mounting heights and downward angles, then testing the result by walking away from the dance floor and listening. The aim is to immerse the people in front of the system without projecting unnecessarily far.
I’m starting to discuss technical guidance with someone who has experience setting up systems that sound good inside while spilling much less outside.

The camp’s position

Speaker A: My main reason for this conversation was to show that we’re willing to be mindful of our neighbours, and always have been. We take the policy seriously, use meters and check our system. We’re willing to do more, including considering limiters and speaker direction.
The current Yellow volume allowance is enough for us, and we wouldn’t mind a 04:00 curfew. We want everyone to enjoy themselves. We have placement requirements already known to City Planning, but those are a separate discussion.
If we had been labelled Orange last year, nothing physically would have changed. Placement has a major role. Still, it’s good to bring sound back onto the agenda so everyone, including us, pays more attention.
Speaker B: Clearer guidance should support people who want quiet and people who want to party within the agreed limits. Both need a place. From this conversation, placement and orientation seem central to the conflict. Camps also evolve, and their activities may eventually fit another zone better.

Warnings and accountability

Speaker B: The proposed warning system builds on existing mediation. If mediation fails because people won’t cooperate, or nobody responsible can be found, the Malfare Lead or Site Lead could attend and issue a warning.
Crews need someone available who can turn the system down or stop it, including when visiting DJs are playing. If another intervention is needed because the problem hasn’t changed, a second warning could follow. From that point, the lead could decide to cut power, but it wouldn’t be automatic.
Warnings and incidents would be recorded so we can recognize repeated problems across events. Recording doesn’t necessarily mean sanctions: it gives us the awareness needed to address a recurring issue.
Speaker A: I understand the intention, but I’d be careful about creating a semi-legal or punitive system. Apart from speaker direction, we believed we were complying with the policy, and we want to continue doing so.
Records should include who is complaining and the context, not only interventions against the sound source. If complaints repeatedly come from the same person or camp, perhaps their placement is also worth discussing. It works both ways.
I strongly favour mutual understanding, although that can be difficult when people start with very different expectations. Clarifying what the policy means is useful.
Speaker B: Power has already been cut in some situations, but without a clear procedure that can feel subjective or be open to dispute. A process should also protect against someone using authority unfairly.
We should record the whole incident: who raised it, what happened, any warnings and the response. For recurring issues, discussion would come before sanctions. One influence is an approach that helps people understand how their actions affected others, while retaining the capacity for consequences if necessary.
Possible responses discussed include moving to a more suitable zone or, for art projects, grant restrictions. These aren’t settled measures.
Speaker A: I’d be cautious with the analogy to consent processes. Even when we comply with the sound rules, complaints can still happen. The discussion must remain two-way: is our placement appropriate, and is the other group’s placement appropriate?
Speaker B: Agreed. Measurements and incident records can establish whether the rules were followed. A complaint doesn’t automatically become a warning. Warnings concern unresolved problems, a lack of cooperation or nobody available to take responsibility.
Speaker A: And even when everyone follows the rules, we still want a conversation about how both groups can be comfortable and have their place at the event. That conversation doesn’t stop at the policy.

Early communication and registration

Speaker B: The barrio registration form would contain a more substantial sound section. Before placement, people would see the zones, dB limits, hours and previous year’s map, with a clear description of what each zone is for.
We’d ask what they plan to do and where they want to go. The answers can be checked against each other. If the plans don’t match the preferred zone, we can discuss it before placement. People would still choose, but understand the limits they’re choosing.
I like self-placement and want to support it with clearer information, rather than start placing everyone ourselves. Registration is when people begin thinking about their location, activities and camp layout. Giving them this information months later is too late.
We should also ask how they plan to reduce spill. That starts a conversation for people who haven’t yet considered how their setup affects others, and helps identify what support they need.
Another possibility, to discuss with Barrio Support, is neighbourhood chats so neighbouring camps can communicate more directly. Flexibility requires communication. We want to build bridges strong enough that we don’t burn them as we go wild.
Speaker A: That feels like a good place to end. I’ll read the new version and respond.
