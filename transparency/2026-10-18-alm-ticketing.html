ALM Open Space — Ticketing

Date: Sunday 18 October 2026 (~60 min, in person, with some participants online)
With: Daniel T (president, facilitating), Daniela (Production, timekeeper), Peter D (treasurer), Chris O, Ant H, and other ALM participants An open-space session at the Annual Leadership Meeting (ALM, 15–19 October 2026), filed under the Finance 2027 working group. It is an open discussion, not a decision-making body. The ideas, prices and numbers below were raised for discussion; none of them is a decision. They have been handed on to the Finance 2027 working group, which will develop them with the community.

A loosely structured session on how the association raises the money to hold its event: tickets, and possibly other models. It began with a round in which anyone with an idea had two minutes, followed by an open conversation. The main themes were low-income access, refunds and transfers, tickets for vendors, artists, crews and people working at the event, membership-based models, ways to lower the ticket price over time, growth, and a possible discount for younger participants. The session closed with a pointer to the Finance 2027 working group, which meets weekly from Monday 19 October.

1. Framing

  • Daniel T opened the session. The association will have to gather money from the community to hold its event, whether through tickets or another model, such as memberships. With a bank account in place and more time than this year, it can design a better system than the one it had to put together in 2026.
  • Daniel T said that many of this year’s constraints came from uncertainty. Ticket waves were used to bring revenue forward so that suppliers could be booked. If the event reliably sells out a number set in advance, that predictability would make it much easier to decide, for example, how many low-income tickets to offer and how to release them.

2. Low-income access and pricing

  • More low-income tickets. Several participants said there are far fewer low-income tickets than applications, and that people who contribute a great deal during the year had been unable to come. Some also asked that the lower salaries of people living in Spain be taken into account.
  • A somewhat higher low-income price, if it means more tickets. One participant suggested that the low-income price could rise in line with the full price, which would fund more of them; people who use low-income tickets, asked by this participant, had found that reasonable. Others agreed. One participant suggested a low-income price around 1.5 times the current one. Peter D welcomed this: he had assumed the aim was to keep the price low, but if it can rise with inflation, more people could be offered one.
  • Gifting. Ideas included letting people who can pay more fund another person’s low-income ticket, and community crowdfunding to give a free ticket to contributors who have burnt out, as a sign of appreciation.
  • Timing as an inclusion issue. Several participants said the low-income application deadline was very early, and that not everyone can plan or save months ahead. Peter D explained that the low-income window was short this year because the timeline was compressed. The cut-off was also lower than intended because there was no money yet so early in the year. The intention is to make the window longer and to include more low-income tickets in the target.
  • Late low-income tickets. Ant H proposed ring-fencing a small number of low-income tickets (six to ten were mentioned) to be available after the deadline, for example for infrastructure crew members who only commit a few weeks before the event. A participant asked for the same for artists with an art grant who cannot afford a full-price ticket.
  • Eligibility. A participant said a proper questionnaire, like the one used this year, is fairer than a trust-based yes/no question, which people can answer untruthfully, and also asked for some flexibility for individual situations. Peter D said allocation currently uses a lottery with a points system.
  • An alternative model. One participant described a ticketing model developed with others in the community. It would base contributions on what people can actually afford (taking into account, for example, the cost of caring for family members) rather than on income or property alone, using a self-assessment, and would link it to the budget: how many tickets, at what average price, fund what. There was not time to present it fully; Daniel T suggested presenting it to the Finance 2027 working group.
  • Peter D suggested that pay-in-instalments services (Klarna was mentioned) could help people who need to spread the cost of an early ticket.

3. Refunds and transfers

  • A participant raised that low-income tickets cannot be transferred, which leaves people who can no longer come out of pocket.
  • Peter D: tickets sold at a discount are probably not going to be transferable, but refunds are the full intention going forward. The idea is a refund queue: anyone who wants a refund joins the queue, and each new ticket sold refunds the next person in it, as long as tickets are still selling. The queue would probably close about a week before the event.
  • Ant H suggested a direct hand-over within that system: the person giving up a ticket sends a link to a friend, who buys it and so triggers the refund. Peter D thought that reasonable.
  • Daniel T said such a refund pool would make the website the only place to buy a ticket. It would also let people buy early with the security of not losing their money, which brings revenue forward without excluding anyone.

4. Vendors, artists, crews and people working at the event

  • Vendors. Daniela said she would like a standard approach to vendors’ tickets. Some local vendors know the community well and take part in its culture. This year she gave them local codes, which was a first. Two reasons for standardising: legal (the association needs to know who is at its event) and cultural (people on site should be aware of and respect the event’s ways, so that participants feel comfortable).
  • Separate allocations rather than low-income. Peter D would prefer departments or teams (for example infrastructure, production or the art team) to have a ticket budget to allocate to their people, in buckets distinct from low-income, which is “one thing”. This is cleaner from an accounting point of view.
  • Objective criteria. Chris O said that whenever tickets are allocated to particular groups, there must be objective criteria for who receives them, to avoid any risk of favouritism.
  • People working at the event. Daniela raised how to handle tickets for people working at the event, such as crew who may be paid for build and strike and then stay for the event, and coordinators. Are their tickets different, and can they still apply for low-income? She described this as a grey area that needs a conversation.
  • Strike crew. A participant mentioned the reduced ticket price for strike crew that had been floated this year. Peter D clarified that, for legal reasons, it cannot be done as a ticket discount in exchange for work. A different way of recognising strike crew is being worked out.

5. Membership-based ideas

  • Monthly payments towards a ticket. Ant H proposed that people could pay a small monthly amount to the association (twenty euros a month was the example), building up to a ticket. Buying a ticket could also count as a year’s membership. This would spread income across the year and make the cost manageable for people on low incomes.
  • Annual membership with the right to buy a ticket. A participant described other regional burns where a small annual fee (around ten to twenty-four euros) gives the right to buy a ticket.
  • A one-off “lifetime” ticket. A participant suggested offering, to those who want it, tickets for life for a large one-off payment (five thousand euros was mentioned), as a way to raise funds at any time of year. Another participant added that it should be conditional on continued good conduct, including respect for consent.
  • Points raised in response. Daniel T noted that the association’s bylaws provide for membership fees. Peter D said they do, but he would not introduce them for 2027: many small transactions are a significant accounting load, and there is a liability aspect. One participant noted that membership fees could only apply to asociados and would have to be the same for everyone, so they could not be optional; a voluntary donation could sit alongside them. Peter D added that nothing can be given in return for a donation.
  • Early access. Daniel T recalled that this year people who had signed up on Humans got early access to the sale. If demand ever outstrips supply, the first week of sales could, for example, be reserved for people with roles or who have volunteered, so that the people building the event can get tickets. He and Peter D did not expect the event to sell out within minutes in 2027.

6. Lowering the ticket price over time

  • Chris O said the general ticket price matters as much as low-income tickets: the event is not cheap for anyone. Bringing the price down, or at least not raising it while inflation catches up, is probably a multi-year project, starting with good stewardship and keeping costs reasonable. A lower price might also leave more room for people to give a little extra.
  • Peter D said that the 2026 event closed with some money left over after costs were cut, which gives a buffer. Lowering ticket prices is one possible use, but that is a decision for the working group. His own rough estimate was that the average price could perhaps come down by about 10% for 2027, while still expanding low-income, and possibly again the year after.
  • Peter D described the budget as a set of levers: ticket price, the number of low-income tickets, the low-income price and others. Moving one up means moving another down, and some depend on forecasting attendance. Some levers can move during the sale; for example, more can go to creativity once more tickets have been sold.

7. Growth and event size

  • A participant said ticketing is also a conversation about growth, and favoured growing gently.
  • Chris O said that, if the tickets can be sold, the event could grow more quickly for the next year or two. Many people who share the culture did not attend in 2026 and might come in 2027, so faster growth now need not threaten the culture; gentler growth could follow. Operationally, he felt a considerably larger event could be supported.
  • Peter D noted that planning discussions have assumed around 3,000 participants, and that he does not expect the event to sell out beyond that in 2027.
  • A participant described another regional burn that links event size to volunteer numbers. Extra waves of tickets are released only when the volunteer roles are filled.
  • Daniel T: reliably selling out is the key to the other levers. Because the association can adapt its own ticketing tools, software is not the limit; how many tickets sell depends on the event’s identity and communication. He added that if the event does sell out, it must be planned so that core contributors (infrastructure, leads, coordinators) can still get tickets.
  • Peter D said he would like to share an internal projection of where sales are likely to end up (with error bars), and how it changes over time, during the sale, to help with planning.

8. A discount for younger participants

  • Peter D raised the idea of a discount for people under 26 (a common European threshold), for community sustainability: a community whose average age keeps rising will eventually struggle to build the event. Adding around 150 younger participants would make a difference, and some would return and bring friends. Other ideas were barrios “adopting” younger participants and bringing younger people into the comms team.
  • A participant asked how many under-26s already attend, to judge how much of a discount would go to existing participants and how much would bring new ones. Peter D said that is a question for the census, and he would like census data to be held in Humans. A participant also asked about the share of first-time participants; that data is not yet available, but the census may have it.

9. Communication and outreach

  • A participant noted that selling tickets depends on the quality of communication. Daniel T pointed to the previous day’s session on event identity. Comms is a department; the event identity work, including the outreach plan, is a project. Part of its aim is to create material that people can share, so that word of mouth spreads further. Those involved are aware that the event’s financial success partly depends on it.
  • On social media, Daniela said she would like to know where the comms team stands on platforms that some community members are not comfortable with. Daniel T’s view was that anyone in the comms team who wants to build a presence on a platform, in a way that fits the event’s identity, should be able to; people who dislike a platform need not use it, but should not block others. Another participant suggested the comms team decide together, and aim each channel at communities adjacent to the culture, such as maker spaces.

10. How decisions will be made

  • Daniel T said these decisions will not be made by the board. They will be made through the Finance 2027 working group, which is open to anyone, will engage with the community (including on community calls), and will work in the open. Board members taking part in a working group does not make it a board decision.
  • Peter D said his main role is to make sure the numbers add up. He will build a model showing what has to change if a lever is moved, and facilitate the conversation, but he does not want to decide which levers to pull.
  • Asked where the discussion of possible paid roles belongs, participants said that who and what (the roles themselves) sit with the organisational structure working group. The finance working group would look at how much of the budget could go to paid roles.

Outcomes

  • An open discussion; no decisions were taken. Prices and numbers mentioned are ideas only.
  • Broad agreement that more low-income tickets are needed, and openness among participants to a somewhat higher low-income price if it means more tickets. Many also want a longer application window and some late or reserved low-income tickets.
  • Support for a refund queue through the official sale as the way to handle people who cannot come, rather than resale.
  • Tickets for vendors, crews, artists and people working at the event should be handled as separate allocations with objective criteria, distinct from low-income.
  • Reliable sell-out was seen as the key to predictability; if demand grows, core contributors’ access must be planned for.
  • All of the above is handed on to the Finance 2027 working group: the general ticket price, low-income numbers and price, refunds and transfers, group allocations, membership-based models, an under-26 discount, event size and growth, and the budget envelope for paid roles.

Next steps

  • The Finance 2027 working group meets weekly on Mondays at 18:00, before the board meeting, starting Monday 19 October. Anyone interested in ticketing and finance is welcome to join — Peter D.
  • The participant who described the alternative ticketing model was invited to present it at the working group.
  • Questions on the roles themselves, including possible paid roles, go to the organisational structure working group.
