Sound Policy Working Group — Transcript

Date: 10 November 2026

Edited English transcript. Opening chatter, interruptions, filler and repetitions have been removed. Personal names and identifying details of camps and projects have been omitted or generalised. Speaker labels are used where the turn is clear from the source. The discussion contains proposals and recollections, not adopted rules. Published in English only.

A simpler policy and a practical guide

Participant A: I’m with you on encouraging people to manage sound themselves and keeping rounds and checks to a minimum. The first document was useful as a starting point. The AI synthesis was too exhaustive, even before I reworked it, but it helped pull the pieces together. Now we can cut it back to what we actually need and make it simpler to read.
Participant B: That helps me understand how the draft came about. The back-and-forth on Discord has got people interested. Several people went and read it, and some wrote to me privately. If people help rewrite the policy, it’ll be easier to start on a shared footing than to end up with a policy people feel they had no say in.
Participant A: Once we have something solid, I want to send it to all the camps and the associados for a round of feedback. The main thing Creativity raised was the vocabulary. It felt bureaucratic to them, as though we were turning the whole thing into a legal process. If that’s someone’s first reaction, it’s hard for them to get to the actual ideas. We should rewrite it in plain language before circulating it again.
Participant B: I could also see the concern about adding work to an already stretched team.
Participant A: From my side, I was imagining a change to a form and a few extra pieces of information, much of which we could provide ourselves. Creativity also shared Borderland’s sound guide in the thread. It made me wonder whether the document people actually use should be a guide. That’s something people might have a reason to update every year, whereas an internal policy could sit untouched for years.
Participant B: What’s the difference between a sound policy and a sound guide?
Participant A: The policy is how we manage sound at Elsewhere: what the rules are, how we respond when they aren’t followed, and who does what across the departments. The guide tells people what they can do and how to do it. Both are useful. We need to work out the policy to write the guide, but the guide might be the thing participants reach for.
Participant B: My worry is adding another long guide. I used to read the Survival Guide, but when it was mostly the same material every year, I stopped. As a barrio lead, I opened the Barrio Guide and it felt long enough that I went back to doing what our established camp already knew. The existing sound policy is easy to grasp: a table, colours, times, decibels, and a direction for your speakers. I can see the value in making it together and giving it more legitimacy, but what I really want to know is what happens when people don’t follow it.
I’ve seen speakers aimed straight at a neighbouring camp. In one case the neighbours asked during build if they could be turned outward and were told they would stay as they were. In another, the answer was that the layout couldn’t be changed anymore. It’s frustrating to make the effort to point your own system outwards and then find that there’s no practical recourse when others don’t.

Declaring sound systems and reviewing the zones

Participant A: That’s why it would help to intervene earlier. I’d borrow the distinction Borderland makes between sound camps and everyone else. If you bring an amplified setup such as a PA, line array or subs, you go through the sound process and fill in the form. An ordinary Bluetooth speaker wouldn’t trigger the full process. We’d need to decide where larger portable speakers such as Soundboks and battery-powered PAs fit; I’d lean towards including them.
The form should show people the zones and what each one allows, then ask what they expect to do and where. Plans can change. The point is to get people thinking about their setup early and give City Planning something to work with. When they place projects, they can assign a direction to each system. Later, if there’s a problem, we can compare what’s happening with what was agreed. A reporting form would give us a record of what happened and how it was handled.
One example of escalation could be: investigate a first complaint and tell the operator; warn them if a substantiated problem continues; and if it still isn’t addressed, stop sound for the night and record the outcome.
Participant B: That sounds harsh if it’s just a count of complaints. Wind can make a big difference.
Participant A: I mean substantiated complaints where the issue isn’t being addressed. If the speakers are pointed the right way and measurements don’t show an excess, but tonight the wind is carrying the sound into green, that’s a different situation.
Participant B: The zones need another look. There’s a big gap between green, where you expect quiet, and yellow, where the graphic makes it look as though music can run until sunrise.
Participant A: I’d like to discuss that with people in yellow, especially where the problems have come up. I’ve heard that the graphic shared on Discord was wrong and that yellow was meant to stop at 04:00, not sunrise. I think 04:00 is already late for yellow. Having conflicting versions in circulation helps explain why people read the rule differently. We could draft revised zones and times and run them past the people affected.
Borderland’s example uses different dB limits across zones and time periods, without a full sound cutoff in the comparison we were looking at.
Participant B: I’m not entirely convinced by that approach. If I sleep in red, I expect noise. If I choose green or blue, I expect to be able to sleep. Even a loud conversation next to a tent or an engine started early to charge batteries makes a difference there.
Participant A: A low limit could also cover loud conversations, rather than only music.
Participant B: A number like 50 dB doesn’t tell me what that feels like.
Participant A: We can come back to how to make that understandable, including the difference between sound people produce and what others experience in a quiet zone.

Sound installations, placement and measurement

Participant A: One proposal is that large sound installations go in red at the perimeter and point outward, like barrios with comparable systems. The hard part is deciding what counts as large. We can ask for RMS wattage, but how do we actually judge the impact of an artwork with sound?
Participant B: If it’s a line array, that probably doesn’t belong on the central playa. But I’m wary of relying too much on dB checks or limiters. What people hear depends on the music and other conditions, too.
Participant A: Whether a limiter is useful depends on how it’s set. Borderland’s guide describes where to measure and includes a bass test file. One interesting detail is measuring a few metres behind the system as well as in front. Even with the speakers aimed in the right direction, low frequencies can travel behind them. At one camp a container behind the system seemed to reduce how much bass reached the playa.
I also like the idea of rewarding mitigation. In the example we discussed, projects that reduced spill could be allowed to play louder or later at certain points in the week. That gives people a reason to look after their neighbours. I’m also interested in having different allowances on weekdays and at the weekend.
Participant B: Two portable speakers on a mobile piece aren’t the same as a large amplified installation. The equipment is part of it, but so is what the artwork does. A small interactive piece playing short bits of music might fit in orange. A large installation with a DJ and a proper PA is a different case. A cinema might be better in yellow than green. It would help to give artists a few examples so they can judge what sort of placement makes sense. Tell them who to talk to if they’re unsure, and that they could be asked to turn down or stop if the setup doesn’t fit the location.
Participant A: And some art was much louder in practice than you would have guessed in advance. Asking about the system could help.
Participant B: They already need to speak to Power if they’re requesting power. It makes sense to ask for wattage and a short equipment list. Four subs tell you something different from a pair of PA speakers, though a pair of speakers and a loud microphone can still be a lot.
Participant A: For a pair of PA speakers on the playa, perhaps we can assign a direction and a limit at a set distance. The centre could have a lower limit than the perimeter, where you can aim outwards. Otherwise, a loud project can drown out other artworks that rely on quieter sound. We could consider something like orange limits for the central playa, even if the surrounding area is designated red. That would give louder projects a reason to choose the perimeter.
I’d keep asking about wattage and intended use. It’s imperfect, but it gives us something to work with.
Participant B: The risk is making a technical field mandatory when someone doesn’t know the answer. I’ve abandoned a form because a compulsory question didn’t give me an answer I could honestly choose. Some artists won’t know the wattage of a small speaker they’re adding to a piece.
Participant A: Ask first what kind of system it is, give examples, and show the technical questions only for larger setups. Someone bringing a substantial PA is more likely to know what the system is rated for or how much power it draws.
It won’t be perfect in year one. There was a cinema that reportedly kept a nearby camper awake, and they had to go and switch it off more than once because they couldn’t find the operator. A contact held by Malfare would have helped resolve it on site. A record would also let us follow up next year: arrange an earlier shutoff or find a better zone for the project. That’s why the reporting work matters.

Sound during build and strike

Participant A: We haven’t really got to build and strike yet. It’s difficult because the usual teams and reporting routes aren’t necessarily in place. The comments on the draft show that people have very different views, even on a suggestion like waiting until 11:00 to use tools.
Participant B: I liked how our own camp handled it, and I wasn’t happy with how a neighbour did. But “we did it right and they did it wrong” isn’t a great basis for a decision, particularly when we’re both from the same camp and the neighbours aren’t on the call. There was loud music until about 05:00 on one build night. That did feel excessive to me.
Participant A: That’s why we need something we can discuss and agree on together.
Participant B: One of the few times I remember this coming up years ago, I was testing a sound system on the Sunday of build and put on a set. People came to dance and let off steam, and it usually finished around 01:00. Then someone came over and told me they had to work the next morning. I didn’t take it well at first, but I turned the sound off. I stopped doing those sets in later years. Some people missed them and others were probably relieved. There might have been a middle ground.
Participant A: I’ve heard complaints in yellow during build, and I’ve also put on parties in yellow and orange myself. Maybe 02:00 is already a generous limit for parties up to and including Sunday. We should make clear when the event rules start; I’d say Monday. If you’re having a party during build, speak to your neighbours first.
Participant B: Yes, that’s what we did. If your neighbours say they need to sleep at midnight, finish at midnight. Even if your immediate neighbours are fine with it, 02:00 could be the hard limit because there are other people around.
Participant A: Speaker direction still matters during build. Even a portable speaker aimed at the next camp can be a problem. It’s easier to get the direction right while things are still being set up.
Participant B: For construction noise, I’d expect people to be able to use tools from sunrise. Some crews arrive late or run into problems and need to work at night. I’d encourage people to think about what can wait, though. You probably don’t need to run an angle grinder at 03:00 if that job can be done in the morning.
Participant A: We could suggest saving the loudest jobs until morning, rather than trying to set a hard rule for every tool. Have you actually had complaints about tools at night?
Participant B: I don’t know of any. We’ve done night work ourselves. Build is a time when people are there to build, so I’d be careful about restricting it without evidence that there’s a problem. With parties, I like having space for spontaneity, as long as people respect their neighbours. If a reporting form shows next year that several people were affected by the same overnight construction noise, we can revisit it. Whatever we write now can evolve.

Who does the work on site?

Participant A: Do we need a sound department, or can we keep the work distributed? In the draft, Creativity and Barrio Support send out the forms and collect the information; City Planning maps the zones and places people; Malfare currently receives complaints and mediates.
Participant B: Could we use sound advisers without building a whole new team? I’d be happy to walk around during build and check speaker orientation. We could have someone technical to call when needed and make meters available. At night I’d lean towards Power, with Site Lead able to take over if Power is dealing with other problems. I’ve only done the night side of that work, so I don’t know how busy the daytime shifts are.
I’m wary of creating more volunteer roles when we already struggle to fill important ones. A few people could help check orientation during build. If the speakers are facing the wrong way, get that corrected before signoff.
Participant A: There’s already a round for permits and structural checks. We could give the people doing it a map with speaker directions and ask them to check orientation. That alone could get us quite far without trying to measure every system.
Participant B: I agree. Unless we decide to fit and seal limiters everywhere and run technical tests, I don’t see a need for a whole sound team. It could be one extra task in the description for each relevant role. I’d rather help existing teams find the volunteers they need.
Participant A: Even if we distribute the work, someone has to coordinate it. The information Barrio Support gathers needs to reach City Planning in time, for example. And someone has to look after the complaints between events, update the policy and deal with recurring problems. That part still needs a home.

Reporting during and after the event

Participant B: We should ask Barrio Support, Creativity and the people who run the census how this could work. I’ve used the census to report a sound problem, including what happened when I tried to resolve it and whether it had happened before. But who reads those answers and follows up?
Participant A: If the census is the right place to report it, the incident still needs to reach the team responsible for dealing with it. Malfare and Site Leads have incident sheets that get entered into a table, but it only captures what was brought to them and written down. There are gaps.
Could we have a simple form open during the event and afterwards? It could collect reports for later review without promising anyone is monitoring it live.
Participant B: What makes the difference is whether someone takes responsibility for following through. When I helped with a camp census, I went through the answers, flagged serious issues and positive feedback, and passed the relevant comments to the right people. For the event-wide census, I don’t know who does that. We should find out.
Participant A: The incident form could be separate from the census, so the teams that need the records can get to them. I’ve started asking the Humans infrastructure team whether we could have a form in Humans. You’d choose a category, such as sound, and it would go to the relevant team. The coordinators could get a notification that a new report is waiting.
Participant B: If it can work in Humans, that makes sense. In the meantime the census could link to the relevant forms, or there could be one place where people find the right reporting route. A forms module might also eventually let us keep the census data in Humans.
Participant A: Consent may want to keep its existing route separate because its information is more sensitive. We don’t have to touch that to make the other routes easier to find.
The same idea could help with the pre-event sound form. Creativity and Barrio Support collect different bits of information now, and I don’t know whether all of it reliably reaches City Planning. Both teams could use the same form, with each team seeing what it needs. Barrio Support could focus on barrios and Creativity on art. City Planning and Power would have the relevant information, and someone checking systems on site could see the applicable declarations together. This would need development; I’m still exploring what’s possible.

Warnings, reachable contacts and stopping sound

Participant A: How should warnings work? In the Borderland example, the operator being unreachable can itself be part of the problem when sound is playing and there’s an issue. Is someone there who can manage the system? If not, can you get hold of the person responsible?
Participant B: You mean when there’s a complaint and a problem to solve, not as a surprise inspection?
Participant A: Exactly. We can discuss a reasonable response window. Making it impossible for anyone to resolve a problem is part of the problem. It might work differently for an installation, an art car and a camp system. For a camp, if you ask a DJ to turn down and they don’t, or they turn it back up after you leave, the next step is to find the responsible person. What happens if nobody can tell you who that is, or you can’t reach them?
I thought Borderland had something like three strikes, but looking at the wording, it’s less rigid than that. There can be a first warning. If the problem isn’t resolved and a second warning is needed, there’s an option to stop sound, after informing the camp and coordinating with Power where a power cut is involved.
Participant B: That seems like a reasonable starting point. If it’s got that far, the issue has already gone beyond what we normally tolerate. Having the option doesn’t mean using it automatically. It depends on what’s happening and whether the operator is working with you.
At our camp, we give DJs a lot of autonomy. We normally check in at changeover, and I’d like to keep that trust without needing someone on a dedicated DJ supervision shift all night. There’s usually someone around who knows how to find one of the sound contacts. If there’s a problem, we can speak to the DJ, stop their set and move on to the next one, or decide not to book them again. Other camps may organise it differently.
Participant A: The main thing is that while sound is playing, there’s someone available who can deal with it or reach the person responsible. They don’t have to be standing in front of the system. They just have to be findable. Don’t leave the sound running with nobody able to intervene.
If it’s a serious problem, I’d expect it to be sorted within an hour. If it isn’t, stopping sound is an option. That doesn’t necessarily have to carry over to the next day. It’s different if the same problem starts up again.
Participant B: It feels rough to stop a whole camp for the night because one DJ at 03:00 wouldn’t cooperate. The DJs at 04:00, 05:00 and 06:00 then lose their sets as well.
Participant A: The camp is responsible for its system. It can give DJs freedom while still briefing the people around them. If a DJ turns the volume back up after a complaint and nobody can reach the lead, the crew should know they can turn the sound down or stop that set. There’s a lot of room to act before anyone has to shut down the whole camp.
Participant B: I think that’s fair. I’d also want whoever handles the complaint to exercise some judgement. I’ve been publicly called out over sound before. We’d shut down the main system but kept playing music through a very small portable speaker. We were technically breaking the rule, but I’d have liked the actual impact and the circumstances to be taken into account.
Participant A: And if it does get to an intervention, the person who first hears the complaint doesn’t have to be the one who stops the sound. That’s a decision for the appropriate lead, such as Malfare Lead or Site Lead.

Recurring problems across events

Participant A: What do we do if the same problem keeps happening year after year?
Participant B: If there’s a real, documented problem with several complaints and people on the ground confirming it, changing the zone could make sense. I wouldn’t treat red as a punishment in itself. It can be a good location for people who want to play louder sound. If people don’t want to sleep there, they could camp separately from their installation.
Participant A: How do we judge when that’s appropriate? We need written reports and someone with the authority to make the decision. Does it sit with Malfare because they receive the concerns, or with a group?
Participant B: Malfare makes sense to consider because sound affects people’s wellbeing. If you choose green expecting to sleep from 02:00 to sunrise and sound from yellow keeps you awake, that matters.
Participant A: So we need incident records, and we still need to decide what would trigger a review and who would handle it.

Participant A: Before we close, could you tell us how limiter setup works at the other event you mentioned?
Participant C: They do a professional sound check, measure at a set distance, then set and seal the limiter. [Some details of this explanation are unclear in the source.]
Participant A: It’s an interesting way to handle it, but it would take a team to check all the systems, which is a substantial ask of volunteers. Let’s talk about it more another time. We’ll continue on Discord and put up another poll for a follow-up call.
