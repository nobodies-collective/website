Sound Policy Working Group — Transcript

Date: 18 November 2026

Edited transcript prepared by the working group. Repetitions, filler, recording formalities, screen-sharing logistics, greetings and unrelated planning/personal discussion have been removed. Names of people, camps and art projects have been anonymized. Speaker labels follow the apparent turns in the transcript; the source did not label speakers. This is an edited transcript, not a verbatim record. Suggestions and numerical examples remain proposals, not adopted rules. References to maps shown during the call cannot fully convey the drawings. Published in English only.

Draft and escalation

Speaker A: I’ve been drafting a second version over the last couple of days. Following feedback about the language, I’ve made it simpler and more straightforward: more of a guide than a policy.
Speaker B: I remember seeing a flowchart in the Borderland guide. Those are easy to follow: what is the problem, what time is it, which zone are you in, and what steps do you take?
Speaker A: I posted a screenshot of their approach in Discord. My proposal stays close to what we already have, with an added warning system. After mediation, if someone isn’t cooperating or nobody responsible can be found, a warning can be issued. If another visit is needed, another warning can follow. From two warnings onward, the Site Lead or Malfare Lead could decide to cut power, with Power’s agreement. It wouldn’t be automatic.
Speaker B: There is a Power Lead on call, but that person might not feel they have the authority to make that decision. It could come from a coordinator, Site Lead or Malfare Lead.
Speaker A: I’d prefer the Site Lead or Malfare Lead to decide, with Power informed and involved.
Speaker B: Yes, Power on call.

Yellow zone and neighbour relations

Speaker A: I want to understand how City Planning works and discuss responsibilities before proposing changes. We’re trying to avoid creating a sound department. I’d also like your perspective on Yellow Zone, as someone familiar with living there.
Speaker B: There seem to be recurring problems when a new camp arrives and one or two people disregard the neighbours. In a previous year, a conflict escalated into name-calling and people unplugging equipment in retaliation. Eventually there was a conversation with the camp lead, but it had become quite intense.
Speaker A: I experienced part of that conflict. As a build lead, I felt I hadn’t been properly informed about the sound rules, and there wasn’t clear communication. We understood we were annoying people at night, but then another camp woke us with loud music every morning. It became unclear whether this was part of the event or simply bad neighbouring.
Speaker B: Some camps use loud music to get everyone up during build. We try to keep it loud enough for our own crew without disturbing neighbours, but people have different habits. We need to say something about build and strike. Strike is usually short, and most of the sound system has already been dismantled, but there have still been smaller issues. Portable Bluetooth speakers can be very loud now.
Speaker A: There is a build-and-strike section in the new draft.
Speaker B: There is also confusion about the difference between Orange and Yellow. People who really want late-night parties should generally be in Orange. Perhaps Yellow could agree collectively to one exceptional party night, but if every camp chooses a different night, it effectively becomes Orange anyway.
Day parties could be possible in Yellow with neighbours’ agreement, especially because there are workshops happening. People could move their party elsewhere afterward. We need a clear description of the zone’s purpose, alongside decibel limits. Perhaps amplified music should stop at midnight, or even 23:00, rather than continuing at “living-room volume.”
Speaker A: We need both: a measurable limit and a clear explanation of the area’s purpose. A sufficiently low limit would still allow conversation without allowing a party. Yellow is strongly oriented towards workshops and daytime activities. People wanting late-night parties should move towards Orange.

Speaker direction and meters

Speaker A: In one previous conflict, our speakers were pointing towards the centre of the plaza. Barrio speakers should point outward.
Speaker B: That was already a rule, as was having a dB display visible to the DJ. Some camps haven’t followed it.
Speaker A: Without checks, these things gradually disappear. I’m proposing that Barrio Support check speaker direction during the permit round. For Red, and possibly Orange dance floors, they could also check whether the meter is installed properly. The microphone should be six metres in front of the speakers. We previously had ours positioned near speaker height in a place where it wasn’t registering usefully. Meter placement, quality and periodic calibration matter.
Speaker B: One good reference meter could be used to compare camp meters, with a sticker showing the difference. People also use phone apps. I’d expect some variation, although I’m not suggesting we become excessively precise about every reading.

[Editorial note: The discussion included informal estimates of meter and app accuracy. These were opinions expressed during the call, not verified specifications.]

Speaker B: There are also hearing-safety issues with very high levels. Some camps have excellent setups: loud inside the dance floor, but much quieter just outside. Measuring close to the speakers doesn’t tell you everything about sound spill. Technical setup makes a big difference.
Speaker A: I’ve heard systems that sounded excellent inside and barely carried outside. But that requires expertise. Direction is the main issue I want to address early.
Speaker B: I’ve checked direction in the past, and it sometimes led to arguments. People don’t like being told to change their setup after building it, even when the rule was communicated beforehand. The guide should show clearly that speakers face away from the playa and not towards neighbouring camps. Turning sideways into a neighbour isn’t enough.
Speaker A: The placement app should show the intended direction when camps place themselves, so they know before designing their layout.
Speaker B: I made maps with speaker-direction arrows in the past. They showed speakers facing away from the playa without pointing at one another. I can share those examples.
Speaker A: General directions could appear in the app, followed by a more precise direction once the camp’s shape and position are known.

Registration and department ownership

Speaker B: Registration should ask whether camps plan parties, whether they want to play past a certain hour, and their expected volume. Answers could guide them towards an appropriate zone. For example, someone planning louder sound shouldn’t choose a quieter zone.
Speaker A: I want to preserve collaborative placement. The form would explain the zones and limits, show the previous year’s map, ask about planned activities and hours, and then ask where they would like to be placed.
During placement, the team could check whether their plans match their preferred zone. If someone wants parties until 02:00 but chooses a zone with an earlier cutoff, we can ask whether they can comply or would prefer another zone. They retain a choice, but understand what that choice means. We also keep a record of that conversation.
Speaker B: Many of these questions have already been asked through barrio registration. We also asked for a sound lead one year, but removed the question because we weren’t using the information. Much of this could be integrated into existing registration.
Speaker A: The aim is to use the same sound questions for barrios, art installations and art cars. Ideally, a module in Humans would share relevant answers with Power, City Planning and the other teams. Barrio Support would remain responsible for barrios, and Creativity for art and art cars.
Speaker B: Someone still needs to coordinate the form. Speaking from my experience with City Planning, rather than on behalf of the board, I think each department should adopt the relevant sound responsibilities within its own system. They need to feel ownership; otherwise it can get lost in translation.
Speaker A: That is also what Creativity asked for. I can propose the questions and explain what each is used for. If we can automatically share information with the teams that need it, that would help. If not, we should leave departments autonomous in managing their parts. I’m not trying to reinvent everything, but to connect what already exists: better communication, a permit-time check, clearer escalation and records for recurring problems across years.

A practical reference for on-site teams

Speaker B: Close to the event, someone could compile an overview for Site Leads, Malfare Leads and other on-site staff. It would recap the zones, art-car permits, permitted hours and directions, and any agreed exceptions. It should also contain a simple flowchart for responding to problems.
For example: a camp is playing too loudly in Yellow at night; what happens next? Who speaks to them, who warns them, and what happens if nothing changes? A short reference with clear steps would help. The relevant information should also reach barrios before the event, potentially through Humans.
Speaker A: So we need clear, visual guidance that people on duty can quickly refer to when there is a sound problem.
Speaker B: Yes. The lack of a clear procedure has contributed to problems in previous years.

Playa zones and communication

Speaker A: The barrio areas have coloured zones, but central playa doesn’t have the same clear divisions. Creativity gives art cars instructions, but we need clearly mapped sound zones beyond the camps.
Speaker B: Those zones could extend onto the playa, with boundaries designed around the layout.
Speaker A: They should appear in the placement app. We also need to communicate boundaries on site, because drivers may not know when they cross from one zone to another. Could we use signs?
Speaker B: The map in Humans can show a phone’s location, with permission. A transparent sound-zone layer could help drivers see which zone they are in. Physical signs might get lost among the lights, artworks and other signs, especially at night. Art cars should have a printed map and know where to check their location. The sound-zone map could also appear in the survival guide and final event map, clearly stating that it applies to art cars.

Large art installations and art cars

Speaker A: Art cars already receive permits through Creativity. I’d like designated stopping places for large systems in the Red perimeter area, facing outward. They could travel elsewhere with lower sound, but loud sessions would happen in those places.
There should be a difference between Red on central playa and Red in the barrio area, and similarly for Orange. Being able to point outward changes the impact. I experienced an art car whose sound overwhelmed our own DJ booth from across the playa. Its speakers were high up and pointing in several directions.
Speaker B: We could add practical art-car guidance on speaker height, angle and direction. We don’t want speakers blasting in all directions from high above the vehicle. Perhaps art cars should also have meters.
There may be space between or behind the louder camps for designated art-car areas, with speakers pointing outward. They should remain visible and accessible without overwhelming the camps or crowding central playa.
Speaker A: Large stationary installations that function as clubs should be placed like sound barrios in the Red perimeter area. An installation shouldn’t be able to play as loudly as a Red barrio while sitting in the middle of the playa. We could reserve spaces for large installations and large art cars, then communicate those through the art and art-car processes.
Speaker B: Yes, but we also need separation between them. Putting several large systems next to each other could create a mess. A minimum distance from camps could be part of the guidance—for example, 30 metres was one possibility, not a settled distance.
Speaker A: Large stationary dance floors would use the Red barrio area. Large art cars would have designated areas and directions for louder parties, while still being able to travel with quieter sound.
Speaker B: Direction changes when a vehicle turns. Perhaps speakers could rotate or be mounted on hinges so the crew can control their direction.
Speaker A: The guide can state the outcome: don’t point towards Blue, and don’t stop in front of a camp and aim at it without talking to them. How the crew achieves that is their responsibility.

Simple boundaries

Speaker A: I see two possible approaches: coloured zones across central playa with the same hours but lower decibel limits than the barrio areas, or one simpler central-playa zone with lower limits overall.
Speaker B: I favour colours, but the boundaries must be simple and readable. They could follow clear lines or recognizable landmarks, with the app available for checking the exact zone.
Speaker A: Complicated shapes could be difficult to follow, especially while moving.
Speaker B: Agreed. We should simplify them rather than expect people to understand intricate boundaries.
Speaker A: I dealt with a portable-speaker party too close to Green. If people want a proper party, they need to move far enough away from the quiet areas.
Speaker B: Yes. The boundaries should make that clear, and late-night parties near the quieter side need limits.

Placement coordination relevant to sound

Speaker A: What is City Planning’s sequence, and when does it need information from camps and Creativity?
Speaker B: Previously, camps chose a colour and City Planning arranged them, considering neighbours, shared infrastructure and preferences. A proposal went out for responses. The newer system involves camps discussing placement with one another. That has worked reasonably well, but when space is tight, some camps choose a location that doesn’t match their sound zone, and there hasn’t always been a check.
Once barrio positions are broadly established, Power plans lines and coordinates with art placement. Some artworks move to make power connections practical. The sound information needs to fit into those existing interactions.

Next work on the guide

Speaker A: Please read the draft and add comments directly. I’ll integrate what came out of this call and continue working towards a second version. The aim is to send it to barrio leads and discuss it on a community call, provisionally around 2 or 3 December. After agreeing the guide, we would work with each department on implementation, which may lead to further changes.
Speaker B: I can comment now and review it again after you integrate this discussion. I’m also happy to help with a flowchart or practical reference for Site Leads and Malfare Leads.
